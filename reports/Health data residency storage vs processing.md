# Moving only the database buys surprisingly little

The belief that data residency rules control only where data is *stored* is wrong as a general rule, but it is right in a narrow band that matters. Texas SB 1188, Florida §408.051(3), France's HDS v2 hosting certification and the EU's optional EHDS Article 86 fix only the physical location of stored records, as does a storage-keyed assessment trigger in British Columbia. Under those rules the hybrid design (application code and support in AWS London, each customer's Postgres and S3 in its own country) passes where all-London fails. Almost everywhere else that location is regulated, the rule also reaches processing and access. The GDPR treats a UK processor reading an EU-hosted database as a transfer, even when the data is only shown on screen for support. Germany, Italy and Spain regulate where data is processed. Nova Scotia requires data to be "accessed only in Canada", Quebec and Alberta catch data "used" outside the province, and Australia's My Health Records Act bans offshore processing. State cloud policies in South Australia and New South Wales, Health NZ's security framework, an HSE tender and Texas Medicaid contracts all name processing, remote access or support location. By country, the belief is **wrong for Canada and Australia**, **right for US statutes but wrong for the US contracts that usually bind**, **right for France but wrong for Germany, Italy and Spain**, **wrong for Ireland, though Irish law allows London anyway**, and **moot in New Zealand law, which has no residency rule, but wrong in Health NZ practice**. So the hybrid wins clearly only in the US and France. Elsewhere it is either unnecessary, because the law already allows London, or not enough, because the rule or the buyer wants compute and support in-country too. For Poised, this argues for building the planned per-trust "data cell" as a full regional stack, with the same Go Lambdas deployed beside the database, rather than a remote database. It also argues for support tooling that needs no one in London to see patient data, because for the strictest buyers the offshore access is the founder himself.

## Five rules fix only where records sit, and the hybrid passes them

The belief has real support in a few statutes, so it is worth being exact about which ones. Texas Health & Safety Code §183.002(a), added by SB 1188, requires a covered entity to ensure that its electronic health records are "**physically maintained in the United States** or a territory of the United States" ([Texas SB 1188, enrolled](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/SB01188F.htm)). The words "retrieved, accessed, or transmitted" in the same subsection describe *which stored records* are caught; they do not say where access may come from. Subsection (b) limits access by job role ("treatment, payment, or health care operations") and has no geography at all. The storage rule has applied since **1 January 2026**. Penalties run to **$5,000 per negligent violation, $25,000 per knowing violation and $250,000 where PHI is used for financial gain**, with licensing discipline after three violations (same source). The covered-entity definition is borrowed from §181.001, which reaches anyone "storing or transmitting" protected health information, business associates included. Poised itself is therefore plausibly bound, not just its hospital customers ([Tex. Health & Safety Code §181.001](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._health_and_safety_code_section_181.001)).

Florida has the same shape. All patient information "stored in an offsite physical or virtual environment" must be "**physically maintained in the continental United States or its territories or Canada**" ([Fla. Stat. §408.051](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/408.051)). The rule is enforced through a licensure affidavit signed "under penalty of perjury", which makes Florida hospitals very conservative buyers ([Fla. Stat. §408.810](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/408.810)). Practitioners mostly read the Texas rule as storage-only. McDonald Hopkins writes that "offshore access is still permitted", provided it "does not result in storage, copying, or caching of EHR data on non-U.S. servers or devices" ([McDonald Hopkins](https://www.mcdonaldhopkins.com/insights/news/s-b-1188-texas-ehr-law-data-localization-ai-and-access-requirements)), and Buchalter agrees ([Buchalter](https://www.buchalter.com/insights/from-phi-to-ai-what-texas-sb-1188-means-for-healthcare-entities-and-vendors/)). Hall Render dissents: in its view the law "is ambiguous regarding whether the prohibition relates only to storage or would also apply to offshore view-only access" ([Hall Render](https://hallrender.com/2025/08/19/new-texas-law-on-ehr-data-localization-and-ai/)). As of October 2026 there is no Attorney General guidance, no agency rule and no enforcement action.

France's HDS v2 referential (arrêté of 26 April 2024) requires that "l'hébergement physique des données de santé devra désormais être réalisé **exclusivement dans l'Espace économique européen**" ([CMS Francis Lefebvre](https://cms.law/en/fra/news-information/nouveau-referentiel-de-certification-des-hebergeurs-de-donnees-de-sante-hds)). Remote access from outside the EEA only obliges the host to inform the client, and only where the country is *not* adequate under GDPR Article 45. The UK is adequate ([TRIS notification 2023/0682/FR](https://technical-regulation-information-system.ec.europa.eu/fr/notification/25159/print)).

AWS's own certification scope supports this reading. Its HDS certificate covers physical sites, hardware and backup (activities 1, 2 and 6) **only in EEA regions**. Virtual infrastructure, platform and administration (activities 3–5) are certified in **London** too ([AWS HDS](https://aws.amazon.com/compliance/hds/)). A Paris database with London compute therefore looks compatible, under three conditions. Backups must stay in the EEA. Poised will very likely need its own HDS certificate for activity 5, "administration and operation", which hyperscalers usually leave to the SaaS operator ([Qovery](https://www.qovery.com/blog/hds-certified-hosting-providers-compliant-cloud-migration)). And an HDS certifier should confirm that data held in memory on London hardware does not count as "physical hosting".

EHDS Article 86 is the only EU text written explicitly about storage alone. The Regulation "shall not preclude a requirement under national law" that "the **storage** of personal electronic health data… be located within the Union" ([EHDS Art 86](https://streamlex.eu/articles/ehds-en-art-86/)). It is an option for Member States, not a mandate. It covers only the priority data categories, and it applies from **26 March 2027** ([EHDS Art 105](https://streamlex.eu/articles/ehds-en-art-105/)). No Member State has been found using it, and Ireland's Health Information Act 2026 does not. British Columbia's Reg. 294/2021 is the last storage-keyed rule: it requires a supplementary assessment only where sensitive information is "disclosed to be **stored** outside of Canada" ([B.C. Reg. 294/2021](https://free.bcpublications.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/294_2021)).

Two caveats limit how far even these rules help. First, "storage-only" does not mean the code can run anywhere and leave traces. The practitioner reading of Texas forbids any offshore copy or cache, and HDS keeps backups in the EEA. In practice, nothing containing patient data may persist in London: logs, queues, Lambda `/tmp`, message payloads, snapshots and CDN caches all count. Second, **no court or regulator in any of the six markets has ruled on brief in-memory processing abroad while the data rests onshore**. The US, Canadian and Australian research all record the same gap. The statutory wording supports the hybrid, but nobody has tested it.

## Everywhere else, the test is who can read the data and from where

The GDPR does not regulate where data is stored at all. Chapter V regulates *transfers*. The European Data Protection Board says that "**remote access from a third country (even if it takes place only by means of displaying personal data on a screen, for example in support situations, troubleshooting or for administration purposes)**… is also considered to be a transfer". Its Example 11 is almost exactly Poised's hybrid: a third-country processor "remotely accessing, e.g. for support purposes, the data which is stored in the EU… results in transfers" ([EDPB Guidelines 05/2021](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/documents/2023-02/edpb_guidelines_05-2021_interplay_between_the_application_of_art3-chapter_v_of_the_gdpr_v2_en_0.pdf)). Moving the database to Frankfurt, Paris or Dublin does not remove the transfer.

Under the GDPR, both designs are lawful today for one reason only: the Commission renewed the UK adequacy decision on **19 December 2025**. The decision covers UK controllers *and processors*, and it **expires on 27 December 2031** unless extended ([Commission Implementing Decision C(2025) 8771](https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/b36eedab-43b8-46eb-ac30-a8d1e6e69f51_en?filename=Renewal+UK+adequacy+GDPR.pdf)).

GDPR Article 9(4) also lets Member States add "further conditions, including limitations" on health data ([GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)), and several have used it to regulate *processing*. Germany's §393 SGB V says the "**Verarbeitung**" (processing) of health data by cloud services "darf nur" ("may only") take place in Germany, the EU/EEA or an adequacy country ([§393 SGB V](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/sgb_5/__393.html)). Italy requires public administrations' data to be "**trattati** mediante infrastrutture localizzate sul territorio dell'Unione europea", including disaster recovery and CDN ([ACN Regolamento, June 2024](https://dirittobancario.it/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Regolamento-ACN-27-giugno-2024.pdf)). Spain requires that the systems for the "recogida, almacenamiento, **procesamiento** y gestión" of national health system users' data "deberán ubicarse y prestarse dentro del territorio de la Unión Europea" ([Art 46 bis Ley 40/2015](https://www.vademecumlegal.es/administrativo/marginales/donde-deber-ubicarse-los-sistemas-informacion-y-comunicaciones-registro-datos-personales-5743)).

Canada writes access and use directly into statute. Nova Scotia's public bodies and their service providers must ensure personal information is "**stored only in Canada and accessed only in Canada**" ([PIIDPA, S.N.S. 2006 c.3](https://nslegislature.ca/sites/default/files/legc/PDFs/annual%20statutes/2006/c003.pdf)). Quebec's health-information Act requires a privacy impact assessment before a health body entrusts anyone outside Quebec with the task to "recueillir, **d'utiliser**, de communiquer ou de conserver" ("collect, **use**, communicate or keep") its information ([Loi 5, 2023 c.5](https://www.publicationsduquebec.gouv.qc.ca/fileadmin/gazette/pdf_encrypte/lois_reglements/2023F/79764.pdf)). Alberta requires a written agreement before health information is "stored **or used**" outside the province ([Alberta Health Information Regulation](https://kings-printer.alberta.ca/documents/Regs/2001_070.pdf)).

Australia goes the same way. Its one statutory localisation rule forbids registered My Health Record participants to "**process or handle** the information relating to the records outside Australia", or to "cause or permit" anyone else to ([My Health Records Act 2012, s 77](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2012A00063/latest/text)). The privacy regulator's cross-border test turns on access: there is no disclosure "until the personal information is **able to be accessed or modified** by the overseas recipient" ([OAIC APP Guidelines ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)).

The policy and contract layers say the same thing even more plainly. South Australia allows OFFICIAL: Sensitive data to be "**stored or processed** offshore" only with sign-off from the agency's Accountable Authority, and its ruling explicitly covers "remote ICT support and maintenance (if accessing data)" ([SACSF Ruling 2.1](https://security.sa.gov.au/cyber-security/sacsf/sacsf-ruling-2.1-offshore-data-storage-and-processing)). The New South Wales 2026 Cloud Policy requires agencies to assess "cross-border **storage, processing and access**" ([NSW Government Cloud Policy 2026](https://digital.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-09/nsw-government-cloud-policy-2026-v1.pdf)). In Ireland, HSE tender 21795 made it mandatory that the solution be "hosted, backed-up **and supported** from within the European Economic Area" ([HSE 21795 notice](https://op.europa.eu/en/web/public-procurement/procurement-details/-/procurement/23523b78-bfdc-48d2-8db5-51454b958bc5)). Texas Medicaid contracts passed down to hospitals state that "**No individual is permitted to access information, systems, or deliverables remotely from a location outside the United States**", and they cover "Data processing" and customer support ([Superior HealthPlan, July 2025](https://superiorhealthplan.com/newsroom/important-compliance-with-us-only-performance-and-data-handling-requirements-07152025.html)).

The DOJ's bulk sensitive-data rule also regulates access, defined as "logical or physical access" whatever the security controls. But it applies only to six countries of concern, and **the UK is not one of them** ([28 CFR 202.201](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-28/section-202.201); [28 CFR 202.601](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-28/section-202.601)).

The strongest case for the founder's belief is that much binding law has no location rule at all, for storage *or* processing. For HIPAA, OCR says expressly that the rules "do not include requirements specific to protection of electronic protected health information (ePHI) **processed or stored**… outside of the United States" ([HHS OCR cloud guidance, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/2025/https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/health-information-technology/cloud-computing/index.html)). New Zealand's agent rule says "it does not matter" whether the agent "is outside New Zealand" or "holds the information outside New Zealand" ([Privacy Act 2020 s 11, archived official text](https://web.archive.org/web/20260926101200/https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/31/en/latest/)). Ontario treats giving data to a vendor as "a use by the custodian, and not a disclosure" ([PHIPA s 6(1)](https://ca.vlex.com/vid/use-of-personal-health-869211571)). In those places the belief is moot rather than wrong: there is no residency rule to be about storage, and London is already lawful. Read this way, the "processing counts" finding hurts the hybrid from both sides. Where the law allows London, the hybrid is unnecessary. Where a rule or a buyer bites, the hybrid is usually not enough.

Buyers also discount storage-only residency for another reason: they often mean sovereignty, that is, who can be forced to hand data over. Canada's federal white paper says that "as long as a CSP that operates in Canada is subject to the laws of a foreign country, Canada will not have full sovereignty over its data" ([GC White Paper on Data Sovereignty](https://www.canada.ca/en/government/system/digital-government/digital-government-innovations/cloud-services/digital-sovereignty/gc-white-paper-data-sovereignty-public-cloud.html)). New Zealand's chief digital officer warns that jurisdictional risks "are still present in onshore data centres where IT products and services are owned by offshore vendors" ([GCDO Cloud Jurisdictional Risk Guidance](https://standards.digital.govt.nz/docref/public-service-gen-ai-guidance-cloud-jurisdictional-risk/2024/en/)). A buyer with that concern sees a London-processed hybrid as a UK service with a local disk.

## Six verdicts, from statute down to the tender

### United States: the statutes are storage-only, the contracts are not

At federal level, HIPAA permits both designs. It needs a business associate agreement and a Security Rule risk analysis that documents the UK location ([HHS OCR cloud guidance](https://web.archive.org/web/2025/https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/health-information-technology/cloud-computing/index.html)). The state location statutes are those of Texas and Florida, both storage-only. No copycat state law had been found by October 2026 ([RXNT](https://www.rxnt.com/what-healthcare-providers-in-texas-and-florida-need-to-know-about-2026-ehr-data-localization-laws/)).

Access rules arrive by contract instead. Texas Medicaid flow-downs ban offshore access and offshore processing, and Wisconsin Medicaid bars offshore work "that involves access to or disclosure of patient health and related information" ([McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/?p=322505)). Medicare Advantage plans need attestations from offshore subcontractors that "receive, process, transfer, handle, store, or access" PHI ([Shumaker](https://www.shumaker.com/insight/offshoring-patient-data-what-health-care-providers-need-to-know-now/)). Hospital BAAs vary. The University of Houston System's bars storage outside the US without consent and is silent on access ([UH System BAA](https://uhsystem.edu/offices/legal-affairs/contract-administration/contract-documents/miscellaneous-contracts-and-forms/_2025/general-business-associate-agreement.pdf)). UAB Health System's bars offshore storage but states that "**Remote access to data stored within the borders of the United States of America is allowed**" with approved controls ([UAB BAA](https://www.ccts.cme.uab.edu/financialaffairs/images/documents/forms/UAB_Business_Associate_Agreement_5.10.21.pdf)), which blesses exactly the hybrid pattern.

The DOJ rule is a due-diligence question rather than a blocker. Hospitals holding health data on more than **10,000 US persons** will ask whether any owner, staff member or sub-processor sits in a country of concern ([UNC DOJ rule summary](https://research.unc.edu/compliance/library/doj-data/)). The verdict: all-London is unlawful for Texas and Florida hospitals and is blocked by many BAAs elsewhere. The hybrid passes the statutes on their text, and passes storage-only and UAB-style BAAs. It fails Medicaid flow-downs and "no offshore access" BAAs, which only a full US deployment with US-based support satisfies.

### Canada: three provinces reach access or use

PIPEDA has no localisation rule. The federal privacy commissioner treats sending data abroad for processing as "a 'use'… not a disclosure", provided the contract gives comparable protection and patients are told their data "may be processed in a foreign country" ([OPC 2009 Guidelines](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/airports-and-borders/gl_dab_090127/)). Public hospitals, however, sit under provincial law, and the provinces split.

**Nova Scotia** is the strictest regime anywhere in this research. Storage *or access* outside Canada is allowed only with the patient's consent, as a disclosure the Act permits, or by a decision of the head of the public body that it "is to meet the necessary requirements of the public body's operation". Such decisions must be reported to the Minister each year. A corporate service provider that "stores or allows access" in breach faces fines of up to **$500,000** ([PIIDPA, as enacted](https://nslegislature.ca/sites/default/files/legc/PDFs/annual%20statutes/2006/c003.pdf)). London compute and UK support are both access from outside Canada. So the hybrid and all-London both fail by default and need the same head's decision; the hybrid only narrows the request.

**Quebec** triggers the art. 78 assessment and an art. 77 written mandate whenever anyone outside Quebec *uses* the information, so both designs carry identical obligations. Quebec's cloud orientation, effective **20 May 2026**, goes further. "Highly sensitive" public-body data must sit on sovereign infrastructure: the Quebec government cloud, government data centres or a minister-designated public cloud. This includes Protected B data that a chief information officer judges critical ([Quebec Énoncé d'orientations](https://www.quebec.ca/gouvernement/ministeres-organismes/cybersecurite-numerique/publications/architecture-entreprise-gouvernementale/enonce-orientations-infonuagique-hebergement)). If Santé Québec classifies pre-op data that way, no design run by a UK vendor clearly qualifies, not even a full deployment in an AWS Canadian region.

**Alberta** asks only for a contract. Its rule is keyed to the provincial border, so a Canadian-region database outside Alberta triggers it as well.

**British Columbia** repealed its old "stored and accessed only in Canada" rule in November 2021 ([BC FIPPA](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/96165_00_multi)). Its storage-keyed supplementary assessment is the one Canadian rule where the hybrid has a clear edge. Even so, the BC privacy commissioner frames the security duty around where information is "processed or stored". One factor it weighs is "whether a reasonable alternative is available within Canada" ([BC OIPC guidance](https://oipc.bc.ca/guidance-documents/3646)).

**Ontario** permits both designs by contract. **New Brunswick** has a regulation section headed "Personal health information stored outside Canada", whose text could not be verified; read it before any New Brunswick deal ([NB Reg 2010-112](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/dam/gnb/Departments/ag-pg/PDF/RegulationsReglements/2010/2010-112.pdf)). Alberta Health Services tells vendors it is "generally recommended that innovators consider keeping their data operations within Canada" ([AHS Connect Care innovators](https://innovators.connect-care.ca/legal/data-privacy-security-residency)).

The verdict: the belief is wrong for Canada. The hybrid buys little outside British Columbia and RFPs worded around storage. A full in-Canada stack is what clears Nova Scotia and most buyers, provided support is Canada-based or approved break-glass access.

### European Union: France rewards the hybrid, Germany ignores it, Italy and Spain defeat it

Under the GDPR both designs are lawful while UK adequacy stands, and the hybrid changes nothing about the transfer. **France** is the one EU market where the hybrid is decisive: all-London fails HDS v2, and the hybrid passes on location. France's stricter sovereign-cloud regime (SecNumCloud, enforced through SREN Art 31 and Decree 2026-272) binds State administrations and six named bodies; public hospitals are not explicitly listed ([Banque des Territoires](https://www.banquedesterritoires.fr/secnumcloud-6-gip-assujettis-les-donnees-sensibles-enfin-definies)). Where a buyer demands SecNumCloud anyway, it requires EU processing, EU administration and an EU registered office ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/01/Data-Localization-and-the-Sovereign-Cloud-EU-Cloud-Regulations-Explained)). No UK company passes that, whatever the design.

**Germany** regulates where processing happens but accepts adequacy countries, so London passes on location. The obstacles lie elsewhere. §393 requires the processing body to have a German establishment ("Niederlassung im Inland") and, since 1 July 2025, a current **BSI C5 Type 2** attestation ([§393 SGB V](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/sgb_5/__393.html)). Noerr notes it is unclear whether the SaaS vendor itself needs the C5, and doubts the establishment rule is compatible with EU law ([Noerr](https://www.noerr.com/de/insights/neue-anforderungen-an-cloud-computing-dienste-im-gesundheitswesen)). Berlin's hospital law adds conditions: the people processing the data must be under criminal-law secrecy comparable to Germany's, and the controller must notify the Senate administration in advance ([Abgeordnetenhaus Berlin, Drs. 19/0529](https://www.parlament-berlin.de/ados/19/IIIPlen/vorgang/d19-0529.pdf)).

**Italy and Spain:** both designs fail for public hospitals, because those rules require processing in the EU. Spain's adequacy exception for transfers leaves some room for interpretation, which Spanish counsel would need to confirm. Private hospitals in both countries are outside these public-sector rules.

**Elsewhere and next:** Dutch law mandates only NEN 7510/7512 security ([Staatsblad 2017, 446](https://zoek.officielebekendmakingen.nl/stb-2017-446.html)), and no location rule was found for Sweden or Finland. Looking ahead, the Commission's Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA) proposal of 3 June 2026 would require even assurance level 1 providers to "establish and maintain infrastructure, assets, and customer data in the EU" ([Covington](https://www.insideglobaltech.com/2026/06/11/the-eu-cloud-and-ai-development-act-in-depth/)). It is a proposal, not law.

One structural risk hangs over every London-processing design. Germany's §393, Berlin's law and Spain's Art 46 bis accept the UK *only* through adequacy; there is no fallback to standard contractual clauses. If adequacy lapsed, UK processing for those customers would become prohibited outright. The hybrid offers no protection, because the UK compute is the problem.

### Ireland: the law allows London, the HSE may not

Irish law has no localisation rule. The Data Protection Act 2018 s.52 governs *who* may process health data: a health practitioner, or someone with an equivalent duty of confidentiality. It says nothing about *where* ([DPA 2018 s.52](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/eli/2018/act/7/section/52/enacted/en/html)). The Health Information Act 2026 has no storage or processing location rule ([Health Information Act 2026](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/eli/pdf/2026/en.act.2026.0010.pdf)).

Government policy is relaxed too. The 2025 Cloud Computing Advice Note says "There are no central data classification requirements in place except for information defined as 'top secret'", and "Sensitivity of data alone should not prevent use of public cloud" ([Cloud Computing Advice Note 2025](https://assets.gov.ie/static/documents/Cloud_Computing_Advice_Note_2025_-_PDF.pdf)). The HSE's own data protection guidance says "no further steps need to be taken" for UK transfers beyond an Article 28 agreement, while adequacy applies ([HSE Schrems II FAQ](https://healthservice.hse.ie/documents/1098/faqs-in-relation-to-schrems-ii.pdf)).

The constraint bites in procurement. Tender 21795's "hosted, backed-up and supported from within the EEA" clause fails both the hybrid and all-London. Only EU compute with EEA-based support passes. That is one confirmed tender, and how often the clause recurs is unknown. Private hospitals showed no sector-specific rule.

The verdict: the belief is wrong for Ireland, because both the GDPR test and the HSE clause reach access and support. Irish law nonetheless permits London. The hybrid helps only against a clause saying "data stored in the EEA", or a future Article 86 rule, and then only if backups also stay in the EEA.

### Australia: no localisation law, but every location rule counts access

APP 8 exists "to facilitate the free flow of information across national borders" ([OAIC ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)). Its trigger is access. The same OAIC guidance says that engaging an overseas contractor is "in most circumstances" a disclosure. Its worked examples of disclosure include an overseas parent giving "technical and billing support" with "access to its Australian customer database". This makes the hybrid legally identical to all-London. Both are lawful with an enforceable contract that requires APP-compliant handling. The hospital stays accountable for Poised's breaches under s 16C, unless Poised "carries on business in Australia".

Section 77 binds only registered My Health Record participants, not a vendor that never connects. If Poised ever integrated as a portal operator or contracted service provider, it would face up to **five years' imprisonment or 300 penalty units**, and only a full Australian deployment with no offshore handling would work ([My Health Records Act s 77](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2012A00063/latest/text)).

The states regulate transfers *out of the state*, and allow them on grounds of a contract or "reasonable steps" ([NSW Health Privacy Manual](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/policies/manuals/Documents/privacy-section-13.pdf); [Qld IP Act s 33](https://www.legislation.qld.gov.au/view/whole/html/inforce/current/act-2009-014)). Victoria's regulator counts a provider that "stores or processes information outside Victoria" as a transborder transfer ([OVIC IPP 9](https://ovic.vic.gov.au/?p=13934)). So a database in Sydney is itself "outside" Victoria.

The policy layer is a set of risk-approval regimes, not bans: South Australia's ruling, the NSW 2026 Cloud Policy and WA Health's cloud zones. WA, for example, rates "Third Party – Offshore-hosted" services as high risk but allows them after a documented risk assessment ([WA Health Cloud Policy](https://www.health.wa.gov.au/~/media/Corp/Policy-Frameworks/Digital-Health/Cloud-Policy/Cloud-Policy.pdf)). Because these regimes name processing and remote support, both designs need the same approvals. In the private sector, Ramsay, the largest operator, tells patients that outsourced storage "may involve storing that information outside of Australia" ([Ramsay privacy policy summary](https://ramsayhealth.com/en/privacy-policy-summary)).

The verdict: the belief is wrong for Australia. The hybrid gives no legal advantage, only a slightly better story in a risk assessment.

### New Zealand: no residency law at all, but Health NZ assesses processing

The Privacy Commissioner says plainly that "Using offshore cloud providers or other agents to **store or process** your data is not treated as a disclosure under principle 12, so long as the agent or cloud provider is not using that information for their own purposes" ([OPC Principle 12 guidance](https://www.privacy.org.nz/assets/New-order/Your-responsibilities/Sending-information-overseas/2020-10-23-Principle-12-Guidance-A715314.pdf)). Rule 5 of the Health Information Privacy Code treats a service provider's "storing, processing, or destruction" of data as a matter of security safeguards, with no location element ([HIPC 2020](https://www.privacy.org.nz/assets/Codes-of-Practice-2020/Health-Information-Privacy-Code-2020-website-version.pdf)). Both designs are therefore lawful, on one condition: Poised must never use identifiable data for its own purposes, such as product analytics or AI training. Doing so would make Poised a holder of the data in its own right and turn the arrangement into a cross-border disclosure.

Cabinet's Cloud First policy applies to Te Whatu Ora (Health NZ). It allows data classified RESTRICTED or below to be held in public cloud, onshore or offshore, after a risk assessment ([Cabinet minute ERS-23-MIN-0019](https://dns.govt.nz/assets/Documents/2023-Cabinet-minute-Refreshing-the-Cloud-First-Policy-and-Strengthening-Cloud-Adoption-Across-the-Public-Service.pdf); [Cabinet paper](https://dns.govt.nz/assets/Documents/2023-Cabinet-paper-Proposals-for-Refreshing-the-Cloud-First-Policy-and-Strengthening-Cloud-Adoption-Across-the-Public-Service.pdf)). Patient data is classed "MEDICAL IN CONFIDENCE", which sits below RESTRICTED. Health NZ's supplier FAQ confirms "there is no strict prohibition on storing PHI outside New Zealand" ([Health NZ HISF supplier FAQ](https://static.info.content.health.nz/docs/health-pros/topics/cyber-security/FAQ-HISO-10029.4.2025-HISF-for-Suppliers.pdf)).

The assessment itself, though, looks at processing. Hospitals must record each supplier's "jurisdictional limit to which NZ health information is to be shared for **processing, use or storage**" ([HISO 10029.1:2023](https://static.info.content.health.nz/docs/HISO/HISO-10029-1-2023-Health-Information-Security-Framework-Guidance-for-Hospitals.pdf)). Supplier staff working "from a different legal jurisdiction" are a named risk ([HISO 10029.4:2025](https://static.info.content.health.nz/docs/HISO/HISO-10029-4-Health-Information-Security-Framework-Guidance-for-Suppliers.pdf)). Māori data sovereignty guidance adds that iwi and Māori should be involved in decisions about "data-storage locations" ([GCDO Māori interests in public cloud](https://web.archive.org/web/20251205081544/https://www.digital.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/technology-and-architecture/cloud-services/help/maori-interests)). That is a pull towards onshore storage as a preference, not a rule.

The verdict: the hybrid gives a better story on onshore storage, but the same UK exposure. AWS opened its New Zealand region (`ap-southeast-6`) on **1 September 2025** ([AWS News Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/now-open-aws-asia-pacific-new-zealand-region)), so fully onshore hosting is now an easy thing for a buyer to ask for.

## The data cell should carry its own compute

The A-19 data-region plan in [`docs/saas-requirements.md`](../docs/saas-requirements.md) §13 is, in effect, the founder's belief turned into architecture. All code and the control plane stay in London. Each non-UK trust gets a "data cell" of one Postgres database and one S3 bucket. The plan already notes that "deploying the API Lambdas into that region too is a later option". The evidence points to three choices.

**Recommended: make that later option the default meaning of a non-UK data cell.** That is, deploy the same Go Lambdas, the `evaluate` function and the jobs worker next to the database, and keep London as a control plane with no patient data. One design then passes every storage-only rule: Texas, Florida, HDS and Article 86. It also passes the processing rules of Italy, Spain and the compute half of Quebec, and Germany's even if UK adequacy lapses, provided support access is handled as described below. It meets most of the procurement wording found, from "hosted, backed-up" in Ireland to "data operations within Canada" in Alberta and NSW's processing assessment. And it removes the cross-region round trip on every query, which the requirements doc already lists as a cost. The `store.ForOrg(orgID)` seam and the `data_region` column stay exactly right; only what sits behind the seam changes.

**Second: the hybrid as a first step.** This is defensible only if the first non-UK customer is a US hospital outside Medicaid flow-downs, or a French hospital. Even then it needs strict engineering so that nothing persists in London.

**Third: all-London.** This remains legally sufficient for permissive buyers: private hospitals in Ireland, Australia and New Zealand, Ontario, Alberta with its s.8(4) agreement, the Netherlands and the Nordics, and Germany once Poised has a German establishment and C5. Even these buyers will increasingly ask for in-country hosting.

The A-19 design as written also has a storage gap. A-19 keeps **patient login accounts (mobile number or email)** and an `account_records` pointer in the London control plane. Florida's statute covers "**all patient information** stored in an offsite physical or virtual environment" ([Fla. Stat. §408.051](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/408.051)). A contact record tied to one hospital's pre-op service is plausibly patient information there, and plausibly health data for HDS. That is an inference, not a ruling. But it means that even under the storage-only reading, the hybrid as specified stores patient information in London. For any market with a storage rule, the fix is to keep patient contact details inside the data cell and hold only an opaque ID centrally. The same discipline applies to CloudWatch logs, SQS queues, Lambda `/tmp`, SES and SMS payloads, cross-region snapshots, and CloudFront caching of API responses (send `Cache-Control: no-store`).

The strictest access-based rules look past servers to people. Nova Scotia's "accessed only in Canada", Texas Medicaid's ban on remote access from outside the US, the HSE's "supported from within the EEA", South Australia's inclusion of remote support, section 77's "cause or permit" and Health NZ's concern about staff in other jurisdictions all apply to whoever can see the data. A full regional stack still fails all of them if a developer in London can query production. The cheapest fix, and one available now, is **support tooling that works without seeing patient data**. By default it would show only metadata, IDs and error codes. Anything more would go through break-glass access that the customer approves, that expires after a set time, and that is logged.

How far that goes differs by market. Under the GDPR, UK access remains a transfer covered by adequacy. In Nova Scotia it still needs the head's approval unless support is based in Canada. The strictest US contracts need US-only personnel, which means an in-country support partner once such a customer appears.

Several prerequisites remain however the system is hosted: a French HDS certificate, a German establishment and C5 Type 2 attestation, Berlin's secrecy condition, Poised's possible covered-entity status in Texas, keeping owners, staff and sub-processors out of DOJ countries of concern, never using New Zealand data for Poised's own purposes, and a HIPAA risk analysis that documents the UK. Before contracting, local counsel should also settle the questions that could flip a verdict. These are the text of New Brunswick's Reg 2010-112 s.22, whether in-memory processing in London counts as "hébergement physique" for HDS, how far Spain's Art 46 bis reaches, and whether Italy's ACN rules contain any adequacy exception. They also include whether a pre-op tool holds "electronic health records" (a term Texas leaves undefined) or "qualified electronic health records" (Florida's definition lists capabilities a pre-op tool may lack), how Santé Québec classifies pre-op data, and whether CADA, if adopted, covers SaaS. This report is research, not legal advice.

## Conclusion

Storage versus processing turns out to be the wrong axis; law versus procurement is the useful one. Across the six markets, binding law allows all-London hosting for most hospital customers, given the right contract. The exceptions are Texas, Florida, France, Italian and Spanish public hospitals, Nova Scotia (without a head's approval) and any My Health Record integration. Of those, only the first three are satisfied by moving storage alone. The others demand in-country processing or access as well. Those storage-only rules form a small, recent cluster in two markets: Florida in 2023, HDS v2 in 2024 and Texas in 2026. Procurement runs the other way. Quebec's May 2026 orientation, NSW's 2026 Cloud Policy, the HSE tender, Texas Medicaid contracts and the CADA proposal all push towards in-country processing, storage and support. Several buyers look past location to corporate jurisdiction: SecNumCloud's EU-headquarters rule, Quebec's sovereign infrastructure and New Zealand's warning about offshore-owned onshore data centres. No hosting choice by a UK company fully answers that. A hybrid built to satisfy storage-only wording is optimising for the narrowest and least durable set of requirements.

For Poised the change is small in code and large in framing. The per-trust seam is right, but what sits behind it should be a whole regional stack, not a remote database. The first non-UK customer's country and buyer type should decide whether that market gets London, a hybrid or a full cell. The most persistent offshore element in the system is not a Lambda but the sole developer's own access to production data. That makes the best compliance investment available today one that needs no infrastructure: support tooling that never has to see a patient's answers.
