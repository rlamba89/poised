# New Zealand: health data residency, storage vs processing/access

Scope: whether NZ law, regulator guidance or health-sector/government policy requires hospital patient data to be (a) stored in NZ, (b) also accessed or processed only in NZ, or (c) neither, with only cross-border disclosure safeguards. The test case is Poised (UK SaaS vendor). Its compute and support are in AWS London (eu-west-2). Two designs are tested: a "hybrid" with Postgres and S3 in NZ (AWS ap-southeast-6) and the app running from London, and plain all-London hosting. Customers tested: a Health New Zealand | Te Whatu Ora hospital and a private hospital.

Research date: 8 October 2026. Labels used in the findings: **[Law]** = binding statute or code of practice; **[Regulator]** = Privacy Commissioner (OPC) guidance; **[Govt policy]** = Cabinet or GCDO policy binding on government agencies (not on private bodies); **[Health NZ standard]** = HISO standard or Health NZ guidance; **[Practice]** = procurement, contract or market practice; **[Commentary]** = law-firm or press.

Source-access note: legislation.govt.nz returns a bot challenge (HTTP 202/403) to scripted fetches. I read the Privacy Act text from the Internet Archive snapshot of the official page, taken 26 Sep 2026 and showing the Act "as at 01 May 2026" ([archive of legislation.govt.nz](https://web.archive.org/web/20260926101200/https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/31/en/latest/)). The canonical URL is https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/31/en/latest/. digital.govt.nz pages were also read through archive.org snapshots, or through the standards.digital.govt.nz mirror, for the same reason.

## Q1. Privacy Act 2020: IPP 12, s 11 (agents) and any localisation requirement

### Takeaway
The Privacy Act 2020 has **no data-localisation rule**: no requirement to store, process or access personal information in NZ. IPP 12 regulates only **disclosure** to a foreign person or entity. Section 11 says information held by an agent, "for safe custody or processing", is treated as held by the principal. It adds that it "does not matter" whether the agent "is outside New Zealand" or "holds the information outside New Zealand". So a foreign SaaS or cloud provider acting only for the hospital is not a recipient of a disclosure, wherever it stores or processes the data. The hospital stays responsible for it, under IPP 5 / HIPC rule 5.

### Cited Findings
- **[Law] s 11, verbatim (as at 1 May 2026):** "(1) This section applies if an agency (A) holds information for or on behalf of another agency (B) (for example, the information is held by A as a representative or agent of B, or for safe custody or processing on behalf of B). (2) For the purposes of this Act, the personal information is to be treated as being held by B, and not A. (3) However, the personal information is to be treated as being held by A as well as B if A uses or discloses the information for its own purposes. (4) For the purposes of this section, it does not matter whether A— (a) is outside New Zealand; or (b) holds the information outside New Zealand. (5) To avoid doubt, if, under subsection (2), B is treated as holding personal information,— (a) the transfer of the information to A by B is not a use or disclosure of the information by B; and (b) the transfer of the information, and any information derived from the processing of that information, to B by A is not a use or disclosure of the information by A." s 11(1) was replaced on 30 Nov 2022 by the Statutes Amendment Act 2022 s 94. **Covers:** storage ("safe custody") and processing by an agent, in or outside NZ. It deems these not to be disclosures. — [Privacy Act 2020 s 11, legislation.govt.nz via archive.org snapshot](https://web.archive.org/web/20260926101200/https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/31/en/latest/)
- **[Law] IPP 12, verbatim (key parts):** "(1) An agency (A) may disclose personal information to a foreign person or entity (B) in reliance on IPP 11(1)(a), (c), (e), (f), (h), or (i) only if— (a) the individual concerned authorises the disclosure to B after being expressly informed by A that B may not be required to protect the information in a way that, overall, provides comparable safeguards to those in this Act; or (b) B is carrying on business in New Zealand and, in relation to the information, A believes on reasonable grounds that B is subject to this Act; or (c) A believes on reasonable grounds that B is subject to privacy laws that, overall, provide comparable safeguards to those in this Act; or (d) … a prescribed binding scheme; or (e) … privacy laws of a prescribed country …; or (f) A otherwise believes on reasonable grounds that B is required to protect the information in a way that, overall, provides comparable safeguards to those in this Act (for example, pursuant to an agreement entered into between A and B)." **Covers: disclosure only.** It says nothing about where data is stored, processed or accessed. — [Privacy Act 2020 s 22 IPP 12 (archive)](https://web.archive.org/web/20260926101200/https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/31/en/latest/); same text on [OPC IPP 12 page](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-act-2020/privacy-principles/12/)
- **[Law] Definition:** "foreign person or entity means— (a) an individual who is neither— (i) present in New Zealand; nor (ii) ordinarily resident in New Zealand: (b) a body, incorporated or unincorporated, that— (i) is not established under the law of New Zealand; and (ii) does not have its central control and management in New Zealand: (c) the Government of an overseas country". A UK company is a "foreign person or entity". — [Privacy Act 2020 s 7 (archive)](https://web.archive.org/web/20260926101200/https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/31/en/latest/)
- **[Law] Extraterritorial application, s 4:** the Act applies to "(b) an overseas agency (B), in relation to any action taken by B in the course of carrying on business in New Zealand in respect of personal information collected or held by B". s 4(2) says "it does not matter— (a) where the personal information is, or was, collected by the agency; or (b) where the personal information is held by the agency". s 4(3) says an agency may be treated as carrying on business in NZ "without necessarily … (b) having a place of business in New Zealand". — [Privacy Act 2020 s 4 (archive)](https://web.archive.org/web/20260926101200/https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/31/en/latest/)
- **[Law] IPP 5, verbatim:** "An agency that holds personal information must ensure— (a) that the information is protected, by such security safeguards as are reasonable in the circumstances to take, against— (i) loss; and (ii) access, use, modification, or disclosure that is not authorised by the agency; and (iii) other misuse; and (b) that, if it is necessary for the information to be given to a person in connection with the provision of a service to the agency, everything reasonably within the power of the agency is done to prevent unauthorised use or unauthorised disclosure of the information." **Covers:** security of storage and of service-provider handling. It sets no location. — [Privacy Act 2020 s 22 IPP 5 (archive)](https://web.archive.org/web/20260926101200/https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/31/en/latest/)
- **[Law] s 23:** "An action taken by an agency in relation to information held overseas does not breach any of the IPPs if the action is required by or under the law of any country other than New Zealand." This assumes information may lawfully be held overseas. — [Privacy Act 2020 s 23 (archive)](https://web.archive.org/web/20260926101200/https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/31/en/latest/)
- **[Law] Breach notification and agents:** for notifiable-breach obligations, "anything done or omitted by an agent of another agency is to be treated as being done or omitted by both the agent and the principal agency" (s 120(4)). "Anything relating to a notifiable privacy breach that is known by an agent is to be treated as being known by the principal agency" (s 121(4)). Definitions of "agent" mirroring s 11 were inserted as s 120(6) and s 121(5) on 27 Nov 2025 by the Statutes Amendment Act 2025 (2025 No 74), ss 126–127. — [Privacy Act 2020 ss 120–121 (archive)](https://web.archive.org/web/20260926101200/https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/31/en/latest/); background in [MinterEllisonRuddWatts, 16 Oct 2024](https://minterellison.co.nz/insights/privacy-act-amendments-reinforce-an-organisation-s)
- **[Regulator] OPC FAQ on offshore cloud:** "We store personal information using a cloud service provider whose servers are held outside New Zealand. Do we need to enter into an agreement with the cloud service provider which incorporates the model clauses? In most circumstances, no – you aren't required by law to enter into such an agreement. This is because, for the purposes of the Privacy Act, you will (in most circumstances) remain responsible for the personal information that you put in the cloud servers… However, the personal information will be treated as being held by Agency A and Agency B if Agency A uses or discloses the information for its own purposes." — [OPC, Sending information overseas](https://www.privacy.org.nz/responsibilities/disclosing-personal-information-outside-new-zealand/)
- **[Regulator] OPC Principle 12 guidance:** "Principle 12 does not apply if the personal information: … is sent to an agent for storage or processing". Also: "Using offshore cloud providers or other agents to store or process your data is not treated as a disclosure under principle 12, so long as the agent or cloud provider is not using that information for their own purposes." Worked example: an NZ company's data "will be stored in data centres outside New Zealand. Overseas Cloud Storage is acting as the agent for NZC… Principle 12 does not apply in this situation as NZC has not disclosed personal information." The guidance names "software as a service provider" alongside cloud providers as agents. — [OPC Principle 12 guidance (PDF, Oct 2020)](https://www.privacy.org.nz/assets/New-order/Your-responsibilities/Sending-information-overseas/2020-10-23-Principle-12-Guidance-A715314.pdf)
- **[Law] Currency:** the Privacy Amendment Act 2025 added **IPP 3A** (notifying individuals when information is collected from someone other than them). It is in force from 1 May 2026, with s 25A excluding information collected before 1 May 2026. IPP 3A covers notice, not location: one item to notify is "the name and address of— (i) the agency that has collected the information; and (ii) the agency that is holding the information". — [Privacy Act 2020 IPP 3A and s 25A (archive)](https://web.archive.org/web/20260926101200/https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/31/en/latest/); [OPC HIPC page on IPP 3A](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-act-2020/codes-of-practice/hipc2020/)
- **[Commentary] Partly contrary framing:** Simpson Grierson (2026) writes that IPP 12 "restricts an agency from disclosing personal information to an overseas recipient unless specified conditions are met". It adds that a hyperscaler's Data Processing Addendum "does not automatically satisfy IPP 12" and that "the rights of the provider to use the data" must be assessed. This fits OPC's view only where the provider uses data for its own purposes (s 11(3)). The firm also says: "Data sovereignty is broader than data localisation. Storing data in New Zealand does not automatically prevent foreign legal access", and "the physical location of the data is not determinative." — [Simpson Grierson, Governing data in the cloud age](https://www.simpsongrierson.com/insights-news/legal-updates/governing-data-in-the-cloud-age-sovereignty-risk-and-resilience-for-new-zealand-boards)

### Inferences
- Under the Privacy Act, NZ is case (c): no storage localisation and no processing/access localisation. IPP 12 covers only disclosures, and s 11(4) expressly takes offshore agents out of the disclosure concept. Poised's London compute, its NZ- or UK-hosted storage and its UK support staff are all "holding … for processing on behalf of" the hospital. That holds only while Poised does not use the data for its own purposes (for example product analytics, AI training or benchmarking on identifiable data). Doing so would trigger s 11(3), making Poised a holder in its own right and the transfer a disclosure subject to IPP 12 / HIPC rule 12.
- Even if IPP 12 were triggered, Poised would probably fit IPP 12(1)(b): an overseas agency selling to NZ hospitals is likely "carrying on business in New Zealand" and so subject to the Act under s 4(1)(b). It could also fit (c), UK GDPR / DPA 2018 as "comparable safeguards", or (f), contract terms such as OPC's model clauses. This is my reading. OPC has not published a ruling on the UK specifically.
- Because of s 4(1)(b), Poised is likely directly subject to the NZ Privacy Act for NZ customer data, including breach-notification liability as an agent under s 120(4).

### Gaps
- legislation.govt.nz could not be fetched directly. The text came from an Internet Archive snapshot (26 Sep 2026) of the official page, "as at 01 May 2026". I found no later amendment to ss 4, 11, 22 or 23, but a change after 26 Sep 2026 cannot be ruled out.
- I could not confirm whether any "prescribed countries" (s 214) or "prescribed binding schemes" (s 213) have been made by regulation. My recollection is that none had been as of 2025; this is unverified.
- OPC's long-promised detailed guidance on cloud/SaaS agents ("The Office of the Privacy Commissioner is preparing more detailed guidance…" in the 2020 PDF) was not located.

## Q2. Health Information Privacy Code 2020: rule 12 and rule 5

### Takeaway
The HIPC replaces the IPPs for health agencies, including public and private hospitals. **Rule 12** mirrors IPP 12: it limits only *disclosure* of health information to foreign persons or entities. **Rule 5** requires reasonable security safeguards and diligence over service providers, and expressly covers "storing, processing, or destruction" by a service provider. Neither rule mentions a location. Section 11 (agents) applies "for the purposes of this Act", so it applies to the code as well. The HIPC therefore adds **no storage or processing localisation**.

### Cited Findings
- **[Law] HIPC scope:** the HIPC "takes the place of the information privacy principles (IPPs) for the health sector" and applies to "all agencies providing personal or public health or disability services". OPC's factsheet lists "hospitals" among health agencies. — [OPC HIPC 2020 page](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-act-2020/codes-of-practice/hipc2020/); [HIPC factsheet 5](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-principles/codes-of-practice/hipc2020/hipc-factsheet-5-storage-security-retention-and-disposal-of-health-information/)
- **[Law] Currency:** HIPC Amendment No 1 (in force 1 July 2022) aligned the code with the Pae Ora reforms and added Health NZ to Schedule 2. Amendment No 2 (March 2026) reflects IPP 3A, in force 1 May 2026. The OPC site hosts the consolidated "Health Information Privacy Code 2020 (in force 1 May 2026)". — [OPC HIPC 2020 page](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-act-2020/codes-of-practice/hipc2020/)
- **[Law] Rule 5, verbatim:** "(1) A health agency that holds health information must ensure— (a) that the information is protected, by such security safeguards as are reasonable in the circumstances to take, against— (i) loss; (ii) access, use, modification, or disclosure that is not authorised by the agency; and (iii) other misuse; (b) that, if it is necessary for the information to be given to a person in connection with the provision of a service to the health agency, including any storing, processing, or destruction of the information, everything reasonably within the power of the health agency is done to prevent unauthorised use or unauthorised disclosure of the information…" **Covers:** security of storage, processing and destruction by service providers. It has no location element. — [HIPC 2020, consolidated version in force 1 May 2026 (PDF)](https://www.privacy.org.nz/assets/Codes-of-Practice-2020/Health-Information-Privacy-Code-2020-website-version.pdf)
- **[Law] Rule 12, verbatim (key parts):** "(1) A health agency (A) may disclose health information to a foreign person or entity (B) in reliance on Rule 11(1)(b) or (c) or 11(2)(a), (c), (d), (f), (i) (j) or (l) only if— (a) the individual concerned or … that individual's representative authorises the disclosure to B after being expressly informed by A that B may not be required to protect the information in a way that, overall, provides comparable safeguards to those in the Act, as modified by this code; or (b) B is carrying on business in New Zealand and … A believes on reasonable grounds that B is subject to the Act, as modified by this code; or (c) … privacy laws that, overall, provide comparable safeguards …; or (d) … prescribed binding scheme; or (e) … prescribed country …; or (f) A otherwise believes on reasonable grounds that B is required to protect the information in a way that, overall, provides comparable safeguards … (for example, pursuant to an agreement entered into between A and B); or (g) the disclosure of the information is in accordance with an authorisation granted under section 30 of the Act." **Covers: disclosure only.** — [HIPC 2020 (PDF)](https://www.privacy.org.nz/assets/Codes-of-Practice-2020/Health-Information-Privacy-Code-2020-website-version.pdf)
- **[Regulator] OPC on rule 5 in practice:** "To comply with rule 5, agencies need to consider what risks there are for the health information they hold, make a plan to address those risks". Also: "Where information is stored electronically, such as in cloud-based storage … or with a third-party provider, health agencies must ensure the records are permanently destroyed including in any back-up system or offsite storage." The factsheet says nothing about data having to be in NZ. — [HIPC factsheet 5](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-principles/codes-of-practice/hipc2020/hipc-factsheet-5-storage-security-retention-and-disposal-of-health-information/)
- **[Regulator] Retention:** "Health Act regulations require all health information held by providers to be retained for 10 years from the last encounter with the patient". This is a retention rule, not a location rule. — [HIPC factsheet 5](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-principles/codes-of-practice/hipc2020/hipc-factsheet-5-storage-security-retention-and-disposal-of-health-information/)

### Inferences
- For a hospital, Poised acting purely as a processor/agent is not a rule 12 recipient. The hospital must still meet rule 5(1)(b): do "everything reasonably within [its] power" to stop unauthorised use or disclosure by Poised and its sub-processors (AWS). This rule applies equally to the NZ-storage hybrid and to all-London hosting.
- Rule 5's explicit mention of "storing, processing, or destruction" shows the drafters had service-provider processing in view. They regulated it through security and diligence, not location.

### Gaps
- No OPC case note, Human Rights Review Tribunal decision or court decision was found that treats offshore storage or offshore processing by a health agent as a rule 5 or rule 12 breach in itself. I did not search case notes exhaustively.

## Q3. Health Information Security Framework (HISO 10029) and Health NZ policy on offshore hosting

### Takeaway
The HISF is Health NZ's security standard, with separate guidance for hospitals (public and private) and for suppliers, including SaaS. It **does not prohibit offshore storage or processing**. Health NZ's own FAQ says "there is no strict prohibition on storing PHI outside New Zealand". The HISF treats location as a **risk and contract item**. Hospitals must record, for each supplier, the "jurisdictional limit to which NZ health information is to be shared for **processing, use or storage**", and assess "jurisdiction where health information resides". Supplier staff working "from a different legal jurisdiction" are a stated risk factor. So in Health NZ practice the processing and access location is assessed alongside storage. It is not prohibited.

### Cited Findings
- **[Health NZ standard] Scope:** "This framework covers the security of all health information that is collected and used within New Zealand; and wherever it is stored. All personal health information is treated as MEDICAL IN CONFIDENCE and given an equal level of protection unless otherwise classified." The footnote ties "IN CONFIDENCE" to the NZ government definition in DPMC Cabinet Office Circular CO (08) 01. — [HISO 10029:2022 Health Information Security Framework (PDF)](https://static.info.content.health.nz/docs/HISO/HISO%2010029%20Health%20Information%20Security%20Framework.pdf)
- **[Health NZ standard] Segments:** "Guidance for hospitals — This guidance applies to public and private hospitals. HISO 10029.1:2023". "Guidance for suppliers — This guidance is for health sector suppliers and their sub-contractors. HISO 10029.4:2025". The supplier guidance covers "outsourced service providers or software as a service (SaaS) providers to the health sector". — [Health NZ, HISF page (last updated 21 May 2026)](https://www.tewhatuora.govt.nz/health-services-and-programmes/cyber-hub/cyber-standards); [Health NZ, Cyber security resources for suppliers (updated 25 May 2026)](https://www.tewhatuora.govt.nz/health-services-and-programmes/cyber-hub/cyber-security-resources-for-suppliers)
- **[Health NZ standard / guidance] Health NZ FAQ, Q05 "Where can Health Information (PHI) be sent or stored? Geographically, what are my obligations?":** "Health Information (PHI) can be stored within New Zealand or overseas, but there are specific obligations to consider: • Data Sovereignty: While there is no strict prohibition on storing PHI outside New Zealand, organizations must ensure that any offshore storage complies with New Zealand privacy laws and standards, such as the Privacy Act 2020. This includes ensuring that the data is protected to the same standards as in New Zealand… • Geographical Obligations: Organizations must ensure that PHI is stored securely, regardless of location, and that access is restricted to authorized personnel." The answer links to OPC's IPP 12 page and the GCDO cloud guidance. — [FAQ – HISO 10029.4:2025 HISF for Suppliers (April 2025, PDF)](https://static.info.content.health.nz/docs/health-pros/topics/cyber-security/FAQ-HISO-10029.4.2025-HISF-for-Suppliers.pdf)
- **[Health NZ standard] Expectation of conformance:** "HNZ expects third parties to align and conform with the HISF… Vendors must comply with relevant laws and regulations, such as the Privacy Act 2020 and the Health Information Privacy Code 2020". — [FAQ HISF for Suppliers (PDF)](https://static.info.content.health.nz/docs/health-pros/topics/cyber-security/FAQ-HISO-10029.4.2025-HISF-for-Suppliers.pdf)
- **[Health NZ standard] Hospital supplier register (HISO 10029.1:2023):** hospitals should record for each supplier "cloud or on-premise", "classification of information which is being hosted", "subcontractors or supply chain details" and the "jurisdictional limit to which NZ health information is to be shared for processing, use or storage". **Covers processing, use and storage explicitly.** — [HISO 10029.1:2023 HISF Guidance for Hospitals, p. 43 (PDF)](https://static.info.content.health.nz/docs/HISO/HISO-10029-1-2023-Health-Information-Security-Framework-Guidance-for-Hospitals.pdf)
- **[Health NZ standard] Hospital supplier risk assessment:** items to consider include "the level of access the supplier has to the hospital's systems and the frequency of access" and "jurisdiction where health information resides at". Agreements should cover "considerations about legal jurisdictions, depending on where the health information is residing". Hospitals "are to be notified of any legal investigations (including e-discovery by their supplier located in other countries on a multi-tenancy environment)… their suppliers may have headquarters overseas and health information may be shared across jurisdictions. This notification requirement is typically documented within a master service agreement (MSA)". — [HISO 10029.1:2023, pp. 43–44 (PDF)](https://static.info.content.health.nz/docs/HISO/HISO-10029-1-2023-Health-Information-Security-Framework-Guidance-for-Hospitals.pdf)
- **[Health NZ standard] Supplier staff in other jurisdictions (HISO 10029.4:2025, under HSUP18):** "Assessing the risks from the supplier staff is especially important when the suppliers have offshore interests or when an individual provides their services for the supplier organisation from a different legal jurisdiction." **Covers remote access and support from abroad, as a risk factor.** — [HISO 10029.4:2025 HISF Guidance for Suppliers (PDF)](https://static.info.content.health.nz/docs/HISO/HISO-10029-4-Health-Information-Security-Framework-Guidance-for-Suppliers.pdf)
- **[Health NZ standard] Supplier design and procurement:** security engineering principles should "clearly state physical and logical security boundaries along with data sovereignty". A product evaluation scheme should cover "the provider's financial stability and jurisdictional residence" and "data sovereignty, interoperability, retention, deletion, and portability". For backups, "If the organisation chooses to implement a cloud-based solution, data sovereignty, jurisdictional and legal boundaries are to be considered" and "perform cloud backups with considerations to data sovereignty and jurisdictional boundaries". — [HISO 10029.4:2025, pp. 74, ~75, 88 (PDF)](https://static.info.content.health.nz/docs/HISO/HISO-10029-4-Health-Information-Security-Framework-Guidance-for-Suppliers.pdf)
- **[Practice/history] Superseded position:** in May 2017 the Ministry of Health "relaxed previous restrictions on the storage of personally identifiable health information in offshore-hosted cloud services", allowing cloud use provided "a risk assessment is undertaken" (secondary source). — [Pulse+IT, 7 May 2017](https://www.pulseit.news/?p=17925)
- **[Practice] Health NZ's own platforms:** Health NZ launched national AWS and Microsoft Azure platforms in Q1 2024, a "$4.5 million hybrid multi-cloud project", hosting parts of the National Data Platform ("hosted in Snowflake on AWS"). The article does not state the regions. — [iStart, 10 Apr 2024](https://istart.co.nz/?p=41610)

### Inferences
- The HISF is the main place where *processing and access location* is named as a governance item: "processing, use or storage", and supplier staff "from a different legal jurisdiction". It is a risk-assessment and disclosure duty, not a ban. A Health NZ assessor will therefore look at **where Poised's compute runs and where its support staff sit**, not only where the database is. In the hybrid design the answer to "jurisdictional limit for processing" is "United Kingdom". NZ storage does not remove the UK from the risk assessment.
- Expect HISF conformance to be required by contract in Health NZ supplier agreements ("HNZ expects third parties to align and conform"). Data-location terms, if any, will come from the specific contract or RFP, not from the HISF.

### Gaps
- I did not find a published Health NZ "offshore hosting policy" or "data sovereignty policy" for vendors. If one exists it may be internal or embedded in contract templates; Health NZ's standard ICT or SaaS contract terms were not available. The older HISO 10064 Health Information Governance Guidelines were not reviewed.
- The AWS/Azure regions behind Health NZ's national platforms were not confirmed from a primary source.

## Q4. NZ Government Cloud First policy, GCDO guidance, classification and NZISM: do they apply to Health NZ, and are there onshore rules?

### Takeaway
The refreshed **Cloud First policy** (Cabinet minute ERS-23-MIN-0019, 4 April 2023) applies to Health NZ, named as "Te Whatu Ora" in the Cabinet paper. It allows public cloud **"whether it's hosted onshore or offshore"** for data classified **RESTRICTED or below**, with only a soft "over time" preference that RESTRICTED information be hosted onshore "where a suitable onshore service exists". Patient health information is IN-CONFIDENCE ("MEDICAL IN CONFIDENCE" under the HISF), **below RESTRICTED**, so it carries no onshore requirement. The policy *does* require a case-by-case **risk assessment**. The GCDO's jurisdictional risk guidance says data is exposed to the laws of wherever providers "**store, process, or transmit**" it. So the policy treats processing location as a risk input, not a prohibition. None of this binds private hospitals.

### Cited Findings
- **[Govt policy] Cabinet minute ERS-23-MIN-0019 (4 Apr 2023), verbatim:** "10 reconfirmed the existing Cloud First Policy, that directs agencies to: 10.1 adopt public cloud services in preference to traditional ICT systems; 10.2 have a plan for how they intend to use public cloud services; 10.3 make adoption decisions on a case-by-case basis following a risk assessment; 10.4 only store data classified as RESTRICTED or below in a public cloud service; 11 agreed that agencies consider accountability, ethics, transparency, and collaboration in relation to Māori data, when making decisions about adopting cloud services; … 14 agreed that over time, RESTRICTED information should be hosted in a New Zealand based data centre, where a suitable onshore service exists". It also "noted the creation of a centralised certification process for onshore hyperscale data centres… to host RESTRICTED and below information" (para 20). — [Cabinet minute ERS-23-MIN-0019 (proactively released by DIA, 16 May 2023)](https://dns.govt.nz/assets/Documents/2023-Cabinet-minute-Refreshing-the-Cloud-First-Policy-and-Strengthening-Cloud-Adoption-Across-the-Public-Service.pdf)
- **[Govt policy] Scope includes Health NZ:** Cabinet paper footnote 2: "Remit of GCDO mandate includes public service agencies as defined in section 10 of the Public Service Act 2020, and entities (Accident Compensation Corporation, Te Whatu Ora, the Earthquake Commission, Kāihga Ora, the New Zealand Qualifications Authority, the New Zealand Transport Agency, New Zealand Trade and Enterprise and the Tertiary Education Commission) covered by the existing ICT direction under section 107 of the Crown Entities Act 2004." — [Cabinet paper, Proposals for Refreshing the Cloud First Policy (2023, PDF)](https://dns.govt.nz/assets/Documents/2023-Cabinet-paper-Proposals-for-Refreshing-the-Cloud-First-Policy-and-Strengthening-Cloud-Adoption-Across-the-Public-Service.pdf)
- **[Govt policy] Ministers' reasoning on jurisdictional risk:** "Jurisdictional risk will very rarely be a conclusive reason to avoid cloud services." And: "We consider the main impact of jurisdictional risk relates to RESTRICTED information. Therefore, we are proposing … a policy preference that agencies use onshore cloud services for storing and creating RESTRICTED information as suitable onshore services become available." — [Cabinet paper paras 34–36 (PDF)](https://dns.govt.nz/assets/Documents/2023-Cabinet-paper-Proposals-for-Refreshing-the-Cloud-First-Policy-and-Strengthening-Cloud-Adoption-Across-the-Public-Service.pdf)
- **[Govt policy] GCDO Cloud First page:** "Only store data classified as RESTRICTED or below in a public cloud service, whether it's hosted onshore or offshore. Over time, host RESTRICTED information in a New Zealand-based data centre, where a suitable onshore service exists." Also: "Make adoption decisions on a case-by-case basis following a risk assessment." The page was last updated 29 Jan 2024. — [digital.govt.nz Cloud First policy (archive.org, Jan 2025)](https://web.archive.org/web/20250129194051/https://www.digital.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/technology-and-architecture/cloud-services/cloud-adoption-policy-and-strategy/cabinet-requirement); live URL [digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/technology-and-architecture/cloud-services/about/cabinet-requirement)
- **[Govt policy] Health information is IN-CONFIDENCE, below RESTRICTED:** the HISF treats all personal health information as "MEDICAL IN CONFIDENCE", citing the government definition of IN CONFIDENCE as information whose release "would be likely to … affect adversely the privacy of its citizens". — [HISO 10029:2022 (PDF)](https://static.info.content.health.nz/docs/HISO/HISO%2010029%20Health%20Information%20Security%20Framework.pdf)
- **[Govt guidance] GCDO Cloud Jurisdictional Risk Guidance (2024):** "Jurisdictional risks can occur where data is subject to the laws of the country where cloud service providers store, process, or transmit data… it's important that agencies engage with cloud providers to ensure they know in which country their data is transmitted, processed, or stored (a concept sometimes referred to as data residency)". Also: "data accessible in a State, which is stored or processed in another State, could be obtained by government agencies in both States." Jurisdictional risks "are still present in onshore data centres where IT products and services are owned by offshore vendors." Providers are assessed on "Location — … where customer data is stored and backed-up, including what points of presence the data may be transmitted, processed or stored." Agencies "must carry out jurisdictional risk assessments when an agency adopts cloud services". They should check "whether providers with data centres in New Zealand have been certified by the Government Chief Digital Officer". The guidance also says: "The use or adoption of cloud services does not hinge solely on consideration of jurisdictional risk." **Covers storage, processing and transmission, as risk inputs.** — [GCDO Cloud Jurisdictional Risk Guidance, 2024 (standards.digital.govt.nz)](https://standards.digital.govt.nz/docref/public-service-gen-ai-guidance-cloud-jurisdictional-risk/2024/en/)
- **[Govt guidance] Archives NZ (Public Records Act):** the "use of cloud based services … does not diminish or remove the statutory responsibilities of public sector organisations in relation to the Public Records Act 2005". Agencies should "Assess, with help of legal experts, the jurisdictional risk of using a cloud provider based off shore" and ask "Where will the information and records be stored/hosted? Which legislation … will the information and records become subject to?" Also: "For organisations with stewardship for iwi and hapū information and records, extra consideration should be given to their location." It sets no prohibition. — [Archives NZ 18/G15 Cloud services: information and records management considerations (v1.2, Jun 2021, PDF)](https://archives.govt.nz/files/etfoy87fj9he/40HgI2o8xFXGiXKue2smHz/fe08a6d73511e85b1d76123d2f1cd65d/18-G15-Cloud-services-Information-and-records-management-considerations.pdf)
- **[Commentary] Press reading of the 2023 refresh:** the ministers think concerns about jurisdictional risk are "overstated". "As it stands, only data up to the restricted classification level can be stored in cloud services. Under the new policy restricted data will be moved to onshore cloud centres." — [Newsroom, June 2023](https://www.newsroom.co.nz/govt-quietly-resets-rules-for-public-sector-cloud-use)

### Inferences
- For a Health NZ hospital, the Cloud First policy permits an offshore SaaS for patient data (IN-CONFIDENCE), stored in the UK or in NZ, subject to a documented risk assessment and Māori-data considerations. The "RESTRICTED should be onshore" preference does not reach ordinary clinical data.
- The GCDO's "store, process, or transmit" framing directly contradicts the idea that residency is "only about storage". In NZ *government risk practice*, the jurisdiction where data is processed or accessible is assessed exactly like storage. In the hybrid design both the UK (compute, support) and the US (AWS parent; CLOUD Act) are in scope, as well as NZ. The hybrid therefore does **not** remove UK jurisdictional exposure. It adds NZ as a further jurisdiction.

### Gaps
- NZISM (GCSB) chapter on public cloud: the site is JavaScript-rendered and could not be fetched. A search snippet (unverified) attributes to NZISM statements that "Cloud services hosted or managed from outside New Zealand pose jurisdictional, data sovereignty, and privacy risks", and that contracts should consider "data sovereignty, jurisdiction, storage and access, including any backups". These are not confirmed verbatim, nor whether they are MUST or SHOULD controls. Source: [nzism.gcsb.govt.nz](https://nzism.gcsb.govt.nz/ism-document/pdf/Section/17478).
- I did not find whether AWS ap-southeast-6 has been certified under the GCDO's Public Cloud Data Centre Certification.
- I did not confirm whether Cloud First was revised again after the January 2024 page update. The archived page (Jan 2025) and the 2024 jurisdictional guidance show no change.

## Q5. Māori data sovereignty (Te Mana Raraunga, Te Tiriti) and how Health NZ applies it

### Takeaway
Māori data sovereignty is **not a statutory localisation rule**. It enters through government policy and practice. Cabinet requires agencies, Health NZ included, to "consider accountability, ethics, transparency, and collaboration in relation to Māori data" in cloud decisions. GCDO guidance says iwi and Māori should be involved in decisions about "data-storage locations". Health NZ's published position is that a Māori data sovereignty framework is still being developed "for Māori, by Māori". In practice a Health NZ buyer may face iwi or IMPB expectations of onshore storage, but no codified rule in this research requires it. Commentators stress that the issue is about governance and control, "not just storage location".

## Q6. Private hospitals (e.g. Southern Cross): sector rules and practice

### Takeaway
Private hospitals are bound by the Privacy Act and the HIPC (rules 5 and 12) exactly as public ones. ## Q7. Is remote access by foreign staff, or processing in another country, treated differently from storage?

### Takeaway
**In binding law, no.** The Privacy Act/HIPC agent rule (s 11) covers any agent "for safe custody or processing", and s 11(4) says it "does not matter" whether the agent is outside NZ or holds the data outside NZ. Remote access by the vendor's overseas staff, acting for the hospital, is therefore not a disclosure. **In government and Health NZ risk guidance, processing and access location are named explicitly** and assessed just like storage. These are inputs to a risk assessment, not prohibitions. This is where the user's belief that "residency is only about storage" is wrong in practice, though right in law.

### Cited Findings
- **[Law]** s 11(1), (4): information held "for safe custody or processing on behalf of B" is treated as B's, and "it does not matter whether A— (a) is outside New Zealand; or (b) holds the information outside New Zealand." — [Privacy Act 2020 s 11 (archive)](https://web.archive.org/web/20260926101200/https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/31/en/latest/)
- **[Law]** s 12: "an action done by, or information disclosed to, a person employed by, or in the service of, an agency in the performance of the duties of the person's employment is to be treated as having been done by, or disclosed to, the agency." — [Privacy Act 2020 s 12 (archive)](https://web.archive.org/web/20260926101200/https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/31/en/latest/)
- **[Law]** HIPC rule 5(1)(b) covers service-provider "storing, processing, or destruction" and sets only a security standard. — [HIPC 2020 (PDF)](https://www.privacy.org.nz/assets/Codes-of-Practice-2020/Health-Information-Privacy-Code-2020-website-version.pdf)
- **[Govt guidance]** GCDO: jurisdictional risk arises "where cloud service providers store, process, or transmit data". "Data accessible in a State, which is stored or processed in another State, could be obtained by government agencies in both States". Risks "are still present in onshore data centres where IT products and services are owned by offshore vendors." — [GCDO Cloud Jurisdictional Risk Guidance 2024](https://standards.digital.govt.nz/docref/public-service-gen-ai-guidance-cloud-jurisdictional-risk/2024/en/)
- **[Health NZ standard]** Hospital supplier register: "jurisdictional limit to which NZ health information is to be shared for processing, use or storage". Supplier guidance: risk assessment "is especially important … when an individual provides their services for the supplier organisation from a different legal jurisdiction." — [HISO 10029.1:2023 (PDF)](https://static.info.content.health.nz/docs/HISO/HISO-10029-1-2023-Health-Information-Security-Framework-Guidance-for-Hospitals.pdf); [HISO 10029.4:2025 (PDF)](https://static.info.content.health.nz/docs/HISO/HISO-10029-4-Health-Information-Security-Framework-Guidance-for-Suppliers.pdf)
- **[Commentary]** "Exposure to foreign legal access can depend on matters such as the jurisdictional connections and corporate structure of the provider, rather than the physical location of the data itself". "The United Kingdom's Investigatory Powers Act 2016 similarly has extraterritorial reach." — [Simpson Grierson (2026)](https://www.simpsongrierson.com/insights-news/legal-updates/governing-data-in-the-cloud-age-sovereignty-risk-and-resilience-for-new-zealand-boards)

### Inferences
- No NZ instrument found draws a legal line that permits offshore *processing* while requiring onshore *storage*, or the reverse. Where location matters (government risk assessment, HISF, Māori data expectations), the instruments look at storage, processing, transmission, access and the provider's home jurisdiction together. A design that stores in NZ but processes in the UK is therefore assessed as UK-plus-US-plus-NZ exposure, not as "NZ-resident".

### Gaps
- No NZ case law or OPC decision specifically on overseas remote-support access to health records was found.

## Q9. Verdict: does each design comply, for a Health NZ hospital and for a private hospital?

### Takeaway
**Both designs are lawful for both customer types.** NZ law and binding codes impose no storage or processing localisation, and a pure-agent SaaS is not making a cross-border "disclosure". The difference is in **policy and procurement**. For Health NZ, Cloud First allows offshore hosting of IN-CONFIDENCE health data but requires a risk assessment that, per GCDO and HISF guidance, looks at where data is stored, *processed* and accessed. It also requires Māori-data consideration, which leans toward onshore storage. The hybrid gives a better "data at rest in NZ" story but the same UK processing and access exposure as all-London. A buyer that truly wants onshore control will want compute and support onshore too.

### Cited Findings
- No localisation in statute; IPP 12 covers disclosure only; s 11(4) covers offshore agents. — [Privacy Act 2020 (archive)](https://web.archive.org/web/20260926101200/https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/31/en/latest/); [OPC Principle 12 guidance](https://www.privacy.org.nz/assets/New-order/Your-responsibilities/Sending-information-overseas/2020-10-23-Principle-12-Guidance-A715314.pdf)
- HIPC rules 5 and 12 have no location element. — [HIPC 2020 (PDF)](https://www.privacy.org.nz/assets/Codes-of-Practice-2020/Health-Information-Privacy-Code-2020-website-version.pdf)
- Health NZ FAQ: "there is no strict prohibition on storing PHI outside New Zealand". — [FAQ HISF for Suppliers (PDF)](https://static.info.content.health.nz/docs/health-pros/topics/cyber-security/FAQ-HISO-10029.4.2025-HISF-for-Suppliers.pdf)
- Cloud First: RESTRICTED-or-below may be in public cloud "whether it's hosted onshore or offshore", and the policy applies to Te Whatu Ora. — [Cabinet minute](https://dns.govt.nz/assets/Documents/2023-Cabinet-minute-Refreshing-the-Cloud-First-Policy-and-Strengthening-Cloud-Adoption-Across-the-Public-Service.pdf); [Cabinet paper fn 2](https://dns.govt.nz/assets/Documents/2023-Cabinet-paper-Proposals-for-Refreshing-the-Cloud-First-Policy-and-Strengthening-Cloud-Adoption-Across-the-Public-Service.pdf)
- Risk lens covers "store, process, or transmit". — [GCDO Jurisdictional Risk Guidance](https://standards.digital.govt.nz/docref/public-service-gen-ai-guidance-cloud-jurisdictional-risk/2024/en/)

### Inferences
Verdict matrix (my analysis, from the sources above):

| | Health NZ hospital | Private hospital |
|---|---|---|
| **(1) Hybrid: DB + S3 in ap-southeast-6; compute + support in UK** | **Law: compliant.** s 11 agent; no IPP 12 / rule 12 disclosure; rule 5 diligence needed. **Policy: permissible** under Cloud First (IN-CONFIDENCE ≤ RESTRICTED), subject to a risk assessment. That assessment will list the UK (processing and support), the US (AWS parent) and NZ as relevant jurisdictions under GCDO and HISF. Helps with Māori-data "storage location" expectations and with any RFP clause saying "data stored/at rest in NZ". It does **not** meet a clause saying "data must not leave NZ" or "processed/accessed only in NZ", because every request moves patient data to London memory. | **Law: compliant.** Same s 11 / rule 5 analysis. No Cloud First. Location terms only if the hospital's policy or contract asks for them. |
| **(2) All-London (eu-west-2)** | **Law: compliant.** Same analysis; UK and Poised likely also meet IPP 12(1)(b)/(c) even if a disclosure were argued. **Policy: permissible** under Cloud First, with a risk assessment. **Commercially weaker:** no onshore storage story for Māori-data or "sovereignty" expectations, and the AWS NZ region (GA 1 Sep 2025) makes full onshore hosting an easy buyer ask. | **Law: compliant.** Practice (e.g. Southern Cross's overseas-transfer notice, AWS-hosted EPR) shows offshore handling is accepted with notice. |

- Contract essentials in either design: (i) Poised processes **only on the hospital's instructions** and never "for its own purposes", which keeps s 11(2) rather than s 11(3) and avoids IPP 12 / rule 12; (ii) rule 5 / HISF controls, including access control and logging for UK support staff, sub-processor list (AWS), breach notice to the hospital fast enough for its duty to "notify the Commissioner as soon as practicable after becoming aware that a notifiable privacy breach has occurred" (s 114; MinterEllisonRuddWatts describes this as 72 hours in practice and recommends that service providers notify principals within 24–48 hours). Agent knowledge is imputed to the principal under s 121(4), and deletion on exit, including backups; (iii) a record of the "jurisdictional limit … for processing, use or storage" (HISF hospital register) and of legal-process or e-discovery notification (HISF MSA item).
- The user's premise needs a correction. For NZ it is **right about binding law**: no rule regulates storage or processing location, only disclosure. It is **wrong about how location is assessed in practice**: NZ government and Health NZ guidance treat processing, transmission and staff access location as residency/jurisdiction factors alongside storage. So "data at rest in NZ" will not be credited as full residency by a careful Health NZ assessor.

### Gaps
- Actual Health NZ RFP or contract clauses on data location (for example "hosted in NZ or Australia" or "no offshore access") were not found. These would decide the commercial question and should be asked of the target customer.
- No source found confirms how Health NZ assessors have, in practice, scored hybrid designs (onshore storage, offshore compute) versus fully onshore ones.
