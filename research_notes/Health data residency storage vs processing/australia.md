# Australia: health data residency (storage vs processing/access) for hospital patient data held by a UK SaaS vendor

Scope and method. These notes cover Commonwealth law (Privacy Act 1988, My Health Records Act 2012), state and territory health and public-sector privacy laws, state government and health department policies, the Commonwealth Hosting Certification Framework, and private hospital practice. Current as at 8 October 2026. Primary text was pulled directly where possible: the OAIC APP Guidelines ch 8, A and B; the My Health Records Act compilation dated 1 July 2026; the Qld IP Act and Tas PIP Act in-force versions; NSW Health's Privacy Manual; the NSW Government Cloud Policy v1.0 (Sept 2026); WA Health Cloud Policy MP 0140/20; the NT Information Commissioner's cloud guideline; and SA SACSF Ruling 2.1. Several state legislation sites (NSW, Vic, WA, ACT, AustLII) blocked automated access, so some statutory wording below comes from official secondary sources (regulator or department extracts). Each such case is flagged.

The scenario tested. A UK company (Poised) runs its application code in AWS London (eu-west-2). For an Australian hospital, the Postgres database and S3 bucket would sit in AWS Sydney (ap-southeast-2). The London code reads and writes over the network, so data is processed in memory in the UK, and UK staff can access it for support. Comparator: everything hosted in London.

## Q1. Privacy Act 1988 (Cth): APP 8, s 16C and OAIC guidance. Is there a localisation requirement? Is overseas vendor access or overseas cloud processing a "disclosure" or a "use"? What changed in 2024–2026?

### Takeaway
The Privacy Act has no localisation requirement. APP 8 does not regulate where data is stored. It regulates "disclosure" to an "overseas recipient", and the OAIC says disclosure happens when an overseas entity is *able to access or modify* the information. So a UK vendor whose servers and support staff can read the data is, on the OAIC's analysis, almost certainly a recipient of an overseas disclosure, wherever the database sits. Such a disclosure is lawful if the hospital takes "reasonable steps" (normally a contract), and the hospital then stays accountable for the vendor's breaches under s 16C. The 2024 amendments added a "prescribed countries" route. The Tranche 2 exposure draft (31 Aug 2026) proposes a controller/processor split but is not law.

### Cited Findings
- **APP 8 obligation (OAIC para 8.2):** "APP 8.1 provides that before an APP entity discloses personal information about an individual to an overseas recipient, the entity must take such steps as are reasonable in the circumstances to ensure that the recipient does not breach the APPs in relation to that information. Where an entity discloses personal information to an overseas recipient, it is accountable for an act or practice of the overseas recipient that would breach the APPs (s 16C)." This governs **disclosure**, not storage. — [OAIC APP Guidelines ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- **The framework's stated purpose is free flow plus accountability, not localisation (para 8.1):** APP 8 and s 16C "generally require[] an APP entity to ensure that an overseas recipient will handle an individual's personal information in accordance with the APPs, and makes the APP entity accountable if the overseas recipient mishandles the information… This reflects a central object of the Privacy Act, to facilitate the free flow of information across national borders while ensuring that the privacy of individuals is respected (s 2A(f))." Chapter 8 contains no statement requiring storage in Australia. — [OAIC APP Guidelines ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- **Meaning of "overseas recipient" (para 8.5):** "a person who receives personal information from an APP entity and is: not in Australia or an external Territory; not the APP entity disclosing the personal information, and not the individual to whom the personal information relates." Para 8.6: sending data to the entity's *own* overseas office is not covered, but a related body corporate overseas *is* an overseas recipient. — [OAIC ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- **Disclosure is triggered by accessibility and loss of control (para 8.8):** "An APP entity discloses personal information where it makes it accessible to others outside the entity and releases the subsequent handling of the information from its effective control." This covers **access**. — [OAIC ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- **Routing through overseas servers is a "use"; the overseas recipient's ability to access or modify is the trigger (para 8.10):** "An APP entity uses personal information where it handles, or undertakes an activity with the personal information, within the entity's effective control. For example, where an entity provides personal information to an overseas recipient, via a server in a different overseas location, there would not usually be a disclosure until the personal information is able to be accessed or modified by the overseas recipient. That is, routing personal information, in transit, through servers located outside Australia, would usually be considered a 'use'." — [OAIC ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- **Overseas contractors: disclosure is the default (para 8.12):** "Where an APP entity engages a contractor located overseas to perform services on its behalf, in most circumstances, the provision of personal information to that contractor is a disclosure." — [OAIC ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- **Overseas technical support with database access is a disclosure (para 8.13, third example):** disclosure where "an Australian organisation relies on its overseas parent company to provide technical and billing support, and as part of this, provides the overseas parent company with access to its Australian customer database (which includes personal information)." This is the closest OAIC example to UK vendor support staff accessing a database hosted in Sydney. — [OAIC ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- **The narrow "use" carve-out for cloud storage (para 8.14):** "in limited circumstances providing personal information to an overseas contractor to perform services on behalf of the APP entity may be a use, rather than a disclosure… For example, where an APP entity provides personal information to a cloud service provider located overseas for the limited purpose of performing the services of storing and ensuring the entity may access the personal information, this may be a 'use' by the entity in the following circumstances:
  - a binding contract between the entity and the provider requires the provider only to handle the personal information for these limited purposes;
  - the contract requires any subcontractors to agree to the same obligations; and
  - the contract gives the entity effective control of how the personal information is handled by the overseas recipient. Issues to consider include whether the entity retains the right or power to access, change or retrieve the personal information, who else will be able to access the personal information and for what purposes, what type of security measures will be used…"

  — [OAIC ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- **Even a "use" leaves the hospital liable for mishandling abroad (para 8.15):** the APP entity "may breach the APPs if the information is mishandled while in the overseas contractor's physical possession… because the APP entity is considered to still 'hold' the information." — [OAIC ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- **"Reasonable steps" normally means an enforceable contract (para 8.16):** "It is generally expected that an APP entity will enter into an enforceable contractual arrangement with the overseas recipient that requires the recipient to handle the personal information in accordance with the APPs (other than APP 1)." The contract should cover purposes, APP compliance (including subcontractors), complaints, and a data breach response plan with notice to the entity. Para 8.17: "More rigorous steps may be required if the information is 'sensitive information'", and health information is sensitive information. — [OAIC ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- **Commonwealth agencies (para 8.18):** s 95B requires contractual measures with contracted service providers, which "will generally satisfy the requirement in APP 8.1." Not relevant to state hospitals. — [OAIC ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- **s 16C accountability (paras 8.60–8.62):** the disclosing entity is "accountable… for an act or practice of the overseas recipient… that would breach the APPs (s 16C(1)). Accountable means that the act or practice is taken to have been done by the APP entity and to be a breach of the APPs by that entity (s 16C(2))." This applies even where the entity "has taken reasonable steps", where a subcontractor breaches, or where the breach is inadvertent. — [OAIC ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- **No s 16C accountability if the overseas recipient has an "Australian link" (para 8.63):** an entity "will not be accountable where… personal information is disclosed to an overseas recipient with an 'Australian link'. A recipient that has an 'Australian link' will be covered by the Privacy Act." — [OAIC ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- **A foreign organisation that "carries on business in Australia" has an Australian link and is itself bound (B.10–B.12, B.20):** "The APPs extend to an act done, or practice engaged in, outside Australia… by an organisation… that has an Australian link (s 5B(1A))." An organisation has one "where it carries on business in Australia (s 5B(3)(b))". "where an entity does not have a place of business in Australia, this does not necessarily mean that it does not carry on business in Australia." The guidance notes the s 5B(3) test changed on 13 December 2022 (B.23). — [OAIC APP Guidelines ch B](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-b-key-concepts); [ch A, A.13](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-a-introductory-matters)
- **2024 amendment: prescribed countries and binding schemes (para 8.28 and fn 17):** an entity may disclose without complying with APP 8.1 where the recipient is "subject to the laws of a country prescribed by regulations, or a participant in a binding scheme prescribed by regulations". The exception "was introduced by the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024. It applies to information disclosed from 11 December 2024." — [OAIC ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- **Other existing exceptions:** a reasonable belief that the recipient is subject to a "substantially similar" law or binding scheme with accessible enforcement (APP 8.2(a), paras 8.20–8.27), or express informed consent (APP 8.2(b), para 8.31). — [OAIC ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- **Tranche 2 (not yet law):** on 31 August 2026 the Attorney-General's Department released an exposure draft, the Privacy Amendment (Personal Data Protection) Bill 2026, with a consultation paper. Submissions closed 18 September 2026. — [HSF Kramer (Sept 2026)](https://www.hsfkramer.com/insights/2026-09/the-draft-tranche-2-privacy-act-reforms-whats-there-whats-new-and-whats-missing); [MinterEllison (4 Sept 2026)](https://www.minterellison.com/articles/draft-tranche-2-privacy-reforms-what-they-mean-for-businesses)
- **The draft introduces controller and processor concepts** to "align Australian privacy laws more closely with the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) regime", and the "go-live date for Tranche 2 reforms has yet to be announced." The MinterEllison summary does not mention any APP 8 change or any localisation requirement. — [MinterEllison](https://www.minterellison.com/articles/draft-tranche-2-privacy-reforms-what-they-mean-for-businesses)
- **Some previously agreed reforms are missing from the draft,** for example removing the small business exemption and a direct right of action. — [HSF Kramer (search summary)](https://www.hsfkramer.com/insights/2026-09/the-draft-tranche-2-privacy-act-reforms-whats-there-whats-new-and-whats-missing)
- **Practitioner reading matches the OAIC:** offshore access by a third party (for example offshore call-centre staff with access to client information) is an overseas disclosure under APP 8. — [Sprintlaw APP 8 guide](https://sprintlaw.com.au/articles/cross-border-data-sharing-australia-app-8-compliance-guide/). This is a secondary source of lower authority, used only as confirmation.

### Inferences
- **The question APP 8 asks is "can an overseas entity access or modify it?", not "where is it stored?"** In the hybrid design, Poised's London servers read and write every record, and UK staff can access data for support. So the UK company is "able to access or modify" the information (8.10), which fits the 8.13 "overseas parent… technical support… access to database" example. Keeping the database and bucket in Sydney does not change the APP 8 analysis compared with all-in-London. Both are an overseas disclosure, or arguably, in both cases, a "use" under 8.14 if the contract gives the hospital effective control.
- **The 8.14 "use" carve-out is framed for storage-only providers.** It covers a provider used "for the limited purpose of… storing and ensuring the entity may access". A SaaS vendor that runs clinical workflow logic and has support access goes beyond pure storage. A cautious hospital privacy officer would therefore treat Poised as a disclosure and require APP 8.1 contract terms. That is achievable: APP 8 never prohibits the arrangement.
- **If Poised "carries on business in Australia"** (for example, contracting with and serving Australian hospitals), it probably has an Australian link. It would then be directly bound by the APPs, and the hospital's s 16C deemed liability would fall away (8.63). The hospital must still take APP 8.1 reasonable steps. This is a legal-advice point.
- **If the Tranche 2 controller/processor model is enacted,** a vendor acting only on documented instructions may be recast as a processor. That would likely make offshore SaaS easier to analyse, but it would not create a localisation rule, based on the summaries reviewed.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm whether any country (including the UK) or binding scheme has yet been prescribed under the new APP 8.2 exception. The OAIC points readers to the Federal Register of Legislation. Check the Privacy Regulation 2013 directly.
- I did not read the Tranche 2 exposure draft text itself, only law-firm summaries. Whether it amends APP 8 or s 16C is not confirmed.
- No OAIC determination was found that applies 8.13/8.14 specifically to a SaaS vendor whose compute is offshore but whose storage is onshore.

## Q2. My Health Records Act 2012 (Cth) s 77: exact wording, who it binds, and whether it binds a pre-op vendor that does not connect to My Health Record

### Takeaway
Section 77 is Australia's one clear statutory localisation rule for health data, and it covers **processing and handling**, not just storage. It binds only four kinds of *registered My Health Record participant*: the System Operator, registered repository operators, registered portal operators and registered contracted service providers. It applies only to records held "for the purposes of the My Health Record system". It does not list registered healthcare provider organisations (hospitals) as such. A pre-op vendor that does not connect to My Health Record and is not registered under s 49 is outside it. Note, however, that if Poised ever became a registered portal operator or contracted service provider, the hybrid design would breach s 77 just as all-in-London would.

### Cited Findings
- **Verbatim s 77 (compilation in force from 1 July 2026):**
  > "77 Requirement not to hold or take records outside Australia
  > (1) The System Operator, a registered repository operator, a registered portal operator or a registered contracted service provider that holds records for the purposes of the My Health Record system (whether or not the records are also held for other purposes) or has access to information relating to such records, must not:
  > (a) hold the records, or take the records, outside Australia; or
  > (b) process or handle the information relating to the records outside Australia; or
  > (c) cause or permit another person:
  > (i) to hold the records, or take the records, outside Australia; or
  > (ii) to process or handle the information relating to the records outside Australia.
  > (2) Despite subsection (1), the System Operator is authorised, for the purposes of the operation or administration of the My Health Record system: (a) to hold and take such records outside Australia, provided that the records do not include: (i) personal information in relation to a healthcare recipient or a participant in the My Health Record system; or (ii) identifying information of an individual or entity; and (b) to process and handle such information outside Australia, provided that the information is neither of the following: [same two exclusions].
  > Fault‑based offence (2A) A person commits an offence if the person contravenes subsection (1). Penalty: Imprisonment for 5 years or 300 penalty units, or both.
  > Civil penalty (2B) A person is liable to a civil penalty if the person contravenes subsection (1). Civil penalty: 1,500 penalty units.
  > (3) This section does not limit the operation of section 99."

  It covers **storage ("hold"), movement ("take"), processing and handling**, and also **causing or permitting** another person to do so offshore. — [My Health Records Act 2012, compilation 2026-07-01, legislation.gov.au](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2012A00063/latest/text)
- **Definitions (s 5):**
  - "registered contracted service provider means a contracted service provider that is registered under section 49."
  - "contracted service provider of a healthcare provider organisation means an entity that provides: (a) information technology services relating to the My Health Record system; or (b) health information management services relating to the My Health Record system; to the healthcare provider organisation under a contract with the healthcare provider organisation."
  - "registered portal operator means a person that: (a) is the operator of an electronic interface that facilitates access to the My Health Record system; and (b) is registered as a portal operator under section 49."
  - "registered repository operator means a person that: (a) holds, or can hold, records of information included in My Health Records for the purposes of the My Health Record system; and (b) is registered as a repository operator under section 49."
  - "registered healthcare provider organisation means a healthcare provider organisation that is registered under section 44."

  — [My Health Records Act 2012 compilation](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2012A00063/latest/text)
- **Hospitals are bound by other MHR obligations, but not by s 77's list:** s 78(1) lists all four groups ("(a) a registered healthcare provider organisation; or (b) a registered repository operator; or (c) a registered portal operator; or (d) a registered contracted service provider") for the My Health Records Rules. Section 77(1) omits registered healthcare provider organisations. — [My Health Records Act 2012 compilation](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2012A00063/latest/text)
- **"Share by default" is now in the Act:** s 41A (prescribed healthcare provider organisations must be registered, civil penalty 250 penalty units) and s 78A (prescribed organisations "must share with the My Health Record system" specified information for specified kinds of healthcare, civil penalty 30 penalty units). — [My Health Records Act 2012 compilation](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2012A00063/latest/text)
- **Secondary confirmation of the reach:** "My Health Records and associated information (e.g., back-ups of My Health Records) must not be held, taken, processed or handled outside Australia at all", except non-personal, non-identifying information held by the System Operator. — [Baker McKenzie Global Data Privacy Handbook, Australia: data localisation (last reviewed 31 Dec 2023)](https://resourcehub.bakermckenzie.com/en/resources/global-data-privacy-and-cybersecurity-handbook/asia-pacific/australia/topics/data-localizationresidency)

### Inferences
- **Section 77 does not bind Poised as currently designed** (no My Health Record connection, no s 49 registration). It does not reach a hospital's own local records just because the hospital is itself a registered healthcare provider organisation: hospitals are not in the s 77(1) list. The restriction attaches to records held "for the purposes of the My Health Record system".
- **Section 77 is the clearest Australian authority against the idea that residency rules are only about storage.** Parliament expressly wrote "process or handle the information… outside Australia" and "cause or permit another person" to do so. If Poised later integrated with My Health Record as a registered portal operator or contracted service provider, only full Australian hosting would work: onshore compute and no offshore support access to that information. The hybrid design would not.
- **Under share-by-default,** if pre-op assessment outputs ever became a prescribed kind of information that hospitals must upload, the hospital (not necessarily Poised) would do the upload. Whether Poised would then become a contracted service provider "relating to the My Health Record system" depends on registration and integration design. Get legal advice before building any integration.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the Australian Digital Health Agency's own guidance on s 77 or on cloud services. The ADHA PDF "Cloud services – Considerations for healthcare organisations" (2020) timed out. That document may explain how s 77 interacts with local clinical systems.
- I did not check whether the My Health Records Rules or the Healthcare Identifiers Act 2010 impose any comparable location rule on conformant software vendors.

## Q3. State and territory health and public-sector privacy laws (public hospitals; private hospitals in NSW, Vic and ACT): any storage-location or access-location rules?

### Takeaway
No state or territory statute requires patient data to be stored in the state or in Australia. Every jurisdiction found uses a **"transfer" or "disclosure" outside the jurisdiction** principle: NSW HPP 14, Vic HPP 9 (and IPP 9), Qld s 33, WA IPP 9, Tas PIPP 9, NT IPP 9, ACT TPP 8. Each permits the transfer on familiar grounds: a substantially similar law, binding scheme or contract, consent, or "reasonable steps". Regulators in Victoria and the NT explicitly say that sending data to a provider that **stores or processes** it outside the jurisdiction is a transfer. Several of these principles are about leaving the *state*, not just Australia. So an AWS Sydney database is itself a cross-border "transfer" for a Victorian, Queensland, WA, SA, Tasmanian or NT hospital, although an easily justified one.

### Cited Findings
**New South Wales (HRIP Act 2002, HPP 14; applies to public *and* private health service providers)**
- HPP 14's operative words: an organisation "must not transfer health information about an individual to any person or body who is in a jurisdiction outside New South Wales or to a Commonwealth agency unless" an exception applies. This is from an exact-phrase search match on the NSW IPC's HPP material; I could not retrieve the full page. — [IPC NSW (media/280)](https://www.ipc.nsw.gov.au/media/280)
- NSW Health's Privacy Manual on HPP 14: it "regulates when NSW health services can transfer personal health information to an agency outside New South Wales, establishing a list of circumstances when this will be authorised. The most useful likely provision to rely on will be where the health service reasonably believes that the recipient of the information is subject to a law, binding scheme or contract that effectively upholds principles for fair handling of the information that are substantially similar to the Health Privacy Principles, or where the patient consents." — [NSW Health Privacy Manual for Health Information, s 13.2](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/policies/manuals/Documents/privacy-section-13.pdf)
- Within Australia (s 13.2.1): sharing "can occur without consideration of the exceptions listed for HPP 14", because the private sector is covered by the Privacy Act and "all states and territories in Australia either have equivalent privacy laws or binding public sector policies". — [NSW Health Privacy Manual s 13.2.1](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/policies/manuals/Documents/privacy-section-13.pdf)
- Outside Australia (s 13.2.2): "needs to be considered on a case-by-case basis. Even where a country has privacy laws, the receiving entity may not be bound by those laws". Exceptions listed: consent; contractual obligation; benefit to the individual; serious threat; "**Reasonable steps** – the health service has taken reasonable steps to ensure that the information that it has transferred will not be held, used or disclosed by the recipient of the information inconsistently with the Health Privacy Principles"; and lawful authorisation. — [NSW Health Privacy Manual s 13.2.2](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/policies/manuals/Documents/privacy-section-13.pdf)
- IPC NSW cloud guidance (search summary): if an agency transfers personal information to a contracted cloud service provider and "the cloud service provider simply holds the data and acts according to the instructions of the agency, then disclosure will not be considered to have occurred." — [IPC NSW guidance (search result)](https://www.ipc.nsw.gov.au/guidance-transborder-disclosure-principle-section-192). I did not verify this wording on the page.
- The HRIP Act binds private health service providers in NSW as well: "Private health service providers in NSW are legally required to manage health information in accordance with the Health Records and Information Privacy Act 2002". — [IPC NSW, For healthcare providers (search summary)](https://ipc.nsw.gov.au/healthcare-providers)

**Victoria (Health Records Act 2001, HPP 9; applies to public and private sector)**
- HPP 9.1, verbatim from an official extract prepared by the then Health Services Commissioner (older version; check against the current authorised version): "An organisation may transfer health information about an individual to someone (other than the organisation or the individual) who is outside Victoria only if— (a) the organisation reasonably believes that the recipient of the information is subject to a law, binding scheme or contract which effectively upholds principles for fair handling of the information that are substantially similar to the Health Privacy Principles; or (b) the individual consents to the transfer; or (c) the transfer is necessary for the performance of a contract between the individual and the organisation…; or (d) …contract concluded in the interest of the individual…; or (e) [benefit to the individual / impracticable to obtain consent / would be likely to give it]; or (f) the organisation has taken reasonable steps to ensure that the information which it has transferred will not be held, used or disclosed by the recipient of the information inconsistently with the Health Privacy Principles; or (g) the transfer is authorised or required by any other law." — [Health Records Act 2001 HPP extract (Office of the Health Services Commissioner), hosted by RCH](https://www.rch.org.au/uploadedFiles/Main/Content/ethics/HPP%20Extract.pdf)
- HPP 10.4 (closure or transfer of a practice) is the only location wording found in the HPPs: a person retaining records "must continue to hold it or transfer it to a competent organisation for safe storage in Victoria". It is limited to the sale or closure of a practice. — [HPP extract](https://www.rch.org.au/uploadedFiles/Main/Content/ethics/HPP%20Extract.pdf)
- OVIC on IPP 9 (Privacy and Data Protection Act 2014, which governs Victorian public sector personal information other than health information): "Where organisations send personal information to a provider that stores **or processes** information outside Victoria, this is a transborder transfer of personal information" (guideline para 9.9, 14 Nov 2019). The guidance does not say IPP 9 prohibits offshore storage. It permits transfers on the IPP 9.1 grounds (for example a contract, with encryption discussed in a case study). — [OVIC IPP 9 guidelines](https://ovic.vic.gov.au/?p=13934); [PDF](https://ovic.vic.gov.au/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/IPP-9-2019.B.pdf)
- Private hospital patients in Victoria have rights under both the Health Records Act and the Privacy Act. — [Fitzroy Legal Service, Law Handbook (search summary)](https://fls.org.au/app/uploads/LHB_2024_Flipbook/1121)

**Queensland (Information Privacy Act 2009, s 33; QPPs now in sch 3)**
- The current in-force text of s 33 ("Disclosure of personal information outside Australia"), as amended by Act No. 32 of 2023: "An agency may disclose an individual's personal information to an entity outside Australia only if— (a) the individual agrees to the disclosure; or (b) the disclosure is authorised or required under a law; or (c) [serious threat]; or (d) 2 or more of the following apply— (i) the agency reasonably believes that the recipient of the personal information is subject to a law, binding scheme or contract that effectively upholds principles for the fair handling of personal information that are substantially similar to the QPPs; (ii) the disclosure is necessary for the performance of the agency's functions in relation to the individual; (iii) the disclosure is for the benefit of the individual but it is not practicable to seek the agreement…; (iv) the agency has taken reasonable steps to ensure that the personal information it discloses will not be held, used or disclosed by the recipient of the information in a way that is inconsistent with the QPPs." — [Information Privacy Act 2009 (Qld), in force](https://www.legislation.qld.gov.au/view/whole/html/inforce/current/act-2009-014)
- Queensland retains a stand-alone s 33 cross-border rule rather than adopting APP 8 inside the QPPs. Section 26 says each QPP is set out in schedule 3. It is unusually strict: it needs agreement, legal authority, a serious threat, *or two* of the (d) limbs. — [IP Act (Qld)](https://www.legislation.qld.gov.au/view/whole/html/inforce/current/act-2009-014)
- Statutory meaning of "disclose" (s 23(1)), which is control-based: the first entity discloses to a second entity if "(a) the second entity does not know the personal information, and is not in a position to be able to find it out; and (b) the first entity gives the second entity the personal information, or places it in a position to be able to find it out; and (c) the first entity ceases to have control over the second entity in relation to who will know the personal information in the future." Section 23(2): "use" includes where the entity "manipulates, searches or otherwise deals with the information". — [IP Act (Qld) s 23](https://www.legislation.qld.gov.au/view/whole/html/inforce/current/act-2009-014)
- Contracted service providers: ss 34–35 require an agency entering a "service arrangement" to "take all reasonable steps to ensure that the contracted service provider is required to comply with parts 1 and 2 and section 41, as if it were the agency". — [IP Act (Qld) ss 34–35](https://www.legislation.qld.gov.au/view/whole/html/inforce/current/act-2009-014)

**Western Australia (Privacy and Responsible Information Sharing Act 2024; IPP 9)**
- Most PRIS Act privacy obligations commenced on 1 July 2026. Mandatory reporting of serious data breaches commences on 1 January 2027. — [PM Lawyers (Jul 2026)](https://www.pmlawyers.com.au/blog/2026/07/health-blog/western-australias-privacy-reforms-begin-on-1-july-2026/); commencement proclamation listed at [legislation.wa.gov.au](https://www.legislation.wa.gov.au/legislation/statutes.nsf/law_s55837_currencies.html)
- WA OIC summary of IPP 9 (Disclosures outside Australia): "An IPP entity must not send personal information overseas unless certain requirements are met. This includes, for example, that the overseas recipient of the information is subject to similar requirements as the IPP entity under the IPPs." Also: "An IPP entity must not send de-identified information overseas unless the recipient has appropriate security in place to protect the information and does not try to re-identify it." The IPPs bind WA agencies, local governments, Ministers, GTEs and "contracted service providers to government". — [WA OIC, IPP summary](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/office-of-the-information-commissioner/information-privacy-principles-summary)

**Tasmania (Personal Information Protection Act 2004, PIPP 9)**
- "9. Disclosure of information outside Tasmania – A personal information custodian may disclose personal information about an individual to another person or other body who is outside Tasmania only if – (a) the personal information custodian reasonably believes that the recipient of the information is subject to a law, binding scheme or contract that has principles for fair handling of the information that are substantially similar to the personal information protection principles; or (b) the individual consents to the disclosure; or (c) [contract necessity]; or (d) the personal information custodian has taken reasonable steps to ensure that the information which it has disclosed is not to be held, used or disclosed by the recipient of the information inconsistently with the personal information protection principles; or (e) the disclosure is authorised or required by any other law." — [PIP Act 2004 (Tas), in force](https://www.legislation.tas.gov.au/view/whole/html/inforce/current/act-2004-046)

**Northern Territory (Information Act 2002, IPP 9; public sector organisations and bound contracted service providers)**
- NT Information Commissioner guidance (May 2017, v1.0): "The Information Act only allows personal information to be sent out of the Territory in the circumstances set out in IPP 9. PSOs should check where a cloud provider operates from, even when dealing with an Australian company. If the provider, **or the hardware used by that provider**, is not located in the Territory (or Australia), PSOs will need to ensure that they comply with IPP 9". IPP 9 may be met "by entering into a contract with the cloud services vendor which provides for the same level of privacy protections as are contained within the IPPs" (citing IPP 9.1(b)). — [NT OIC, Cloud Computing & Privacy Principles Guidelines](https://infocomm.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0017/425510/5-Privacy-Cloud-Computing-May-2017-V1.pdf)
- On use and disclosure, the same guideline says: "If a PSO agreement with a cloud provider allows the PSO to retain control over and sole access to its information, then the transfer… will be a 'use' and not a 'disclosure'. However, if the agreement does not allow the PSO to retain control over the information, or it allows the cloud provider to access the information… this will be a disclosure." — [NT OIC guideline](https://infocomm.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0017/425510/5-Privacy-Cloud-Computing-May-2017-V1.pdf)
- Its project checklist asks about processing and support location, not just storage: "where are the servers?… where will the information travel and who will handle it?… **who provides IT support and where are they located?**" — [NT OIC guideline](https://infocomm.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0017/425510/5-Privacy-Cloud-Computing-May-2017-V1.pdf)

**ACT (Information Privacy Act 2014, TPP 8; Health Records (Privacy and Access) Act 1997)**
- TPP 8 "outlines the steps an agency must take to protect personal information before it is disclosed overseas" (a cross-border disclosure principle modelled on APP 8). — [OAIC, Territory Privacy Principles (search summary)](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-in-your-state/privacy-in-the-act/territory-privacy-principles)
- NSW, Victorian and ACT health records laws "impose specific and additional requirements on private health service providers", on top of the Privacy Act. — [ALRC Report 96 (search summary)](https://www.alrc.gov.au/publication/essentially-yours-the-protection-of-human-genetic-information-in-australia-alrc-report-96/07-information-and-health-privacy-law/state-and-territory-privacy-legislation/)

**South Australia (no privacy statute; Cabinet instruction)**
- SA's Information Privacy Principles were "introduced in July 1989 by means of Cabinet Administrative Instruction 1/89, known as the Information Privacy Principles Instruction". They regulate how SA public sector agencies collect, use, store and disclose personal information. — [ADHA resource page, SA IPPs (search summary)](https://www.digitalhealth.gov.au/resources/sa-information-privacy-principles); [SA AGD, Privacy rights](https://www.agd.sa.gov.au/legal-rights/privacy-rights). SA's binding offshore rule is a cyber-security policy ruling (see Q4).

### Inferences
- Across all eight jurisdictions, **statutory law regulates transfer or disclosure, not storage location.** All of them can be met with a well-drafted contract (the "substantially similar… contract" and "reasonable steps" limbs). Queensland is the strictest wording (two limbs needed under s 33(d)), but a contract plus "necessary for the agency's functions" would usually satisfy it.
- **The control-based tests (Qld s 23, the NT "sole access" test, the OAIC 8.8/8.10 test) turn on whether the vendor can access the data.** The hybrid design gives the UK vendor full application-level and support access, so it is a disclosure or transfer to an overseas entity under these tests, exactly like all-in-London.
- **The state-border point cuts against the "store in Australia" framing.** For a hospital in Victoria, Queensland, WA, SA, Tasmania or the NT, a database in AWS Sydney is itself "outside" the state. HPP 9 and IPP 9 would treat it as a transfer, and so would OVIC's "stores or processes" reading. Australian law treats interstate and overseas transfers in the same structural way: permitted on contract and reasonable-steps grounds.
- **For NSW, Victorian and ACT private hospitals, two regimes apply at once:** Privacy Act APP 8 and the state health records law (HPP 14 or HPP 9). Neither imposes storage localisation.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the authorised current texts of the NSW HRIP Act sch 1 (Cloudflare block), Vic Health Records Act (current consolidation), WA PRIS Act sch 1 IPP 9, NT Information Act sch 2 or ACT Acts. The wording above is from official extracts and summaries.
- I did not verify whether the ACT Health Records (Privacy and Access) Act 1997 contains any cross-border or transborder principle for health records, or whether ACT public health records are governed only by TPP 8.
- I did not find the Victorian Health Complaints Commissioner's current guidance on HPP 9 and cloud.
- The QPP commencement date (generally reported as 1 July 2025) was not confirmed from a primary source in this session. The in-force Act text does show the 2023 amendments and sch 3 QPPs.

## Q4. State government and health department policies (may be stricter than law): NSW, Victoria, Queensland, WA, SA. Do they require onshore hosting and onshore-only access?

### Takeaway
None of the policies found imposes an absolute onshore-only rule for patient data. They are risk-based approval regimes that **explicitly treat offshore processing and offshore access the same as offshore storage**:
- **SA SACSF Ruling 2.1:** offshore storage *or* processing of OFFICIAL: Sensitive data needs a risk assessment and Accountable Authority sign-off, and "remote ICT support and maintenance (if accessing data)" is caught.
- **NSW Government Cloud Policy, 1 Oct 2026:** agencies must assess "cross-border storage, processing and access".
- **NSW ICT contract (ICTA) cl 9.4 location clause:** covers data "stored, processed, accessed, disclosed or viewed" outside NSW, and needs CISO approval.
- **WA Health Cloud Policy:** offshore-hosted services are "high risk" but permitted after risk assessment.
- **Queensland:** the old cloud guide (now repealed) was risk-based.

So the hybrid design gives no policy advantage: these regimes ask where the data is processed and who can access it from where.

### Cited Findings
**New South Wales**
- NSW Government Cloud Policy v1.0: approved 1 Sep 2026, first published 29 Sep 2026. It is mandatory and "Superseded 2020/21 Cloud Policy". The web page lists it as effective 1 October 2026 under circular DCS-2026-04. — [NSW Government Cloud Policy 2026 PDF](https://digital.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-09/nsw-government-cloud-policy-2026-v1.pdf); [policy page](https://digital.nsw.gov.au/policy/cloud-strategy-and-policy/cloud-policy)
- Scope (s 1.2): "applies to all Government Sector Agencies, as defined in the Government Sector Employment Act 2013, that are subject to Ministerial direction and control". - Mandatory requirement (s 2.2.1), verbatim: agencies must "**assess data residency and jurisdictional risks relating to the data, systems and services that support the solution, including cross-border storage, processing and access**". They must also "retain accountability for government data, regardless of deployment or service model, or the role of third-party providers", and "ensure that information classified as PROTECTED or above is stored, processed and transmitted only in environments assessed as suitable for that classification (IRAP assessed)". — [NSW Cloud Policy 2026](https://digital.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-09/nsw-government-cloud-policy-2026-v1.pdf)

### Inferences
- **The policy layer is where location matters most in practice, and it is explicitly not storage-only.** The SA ruling ("stored or processed offshore"; "remote ICT support… if accessing data"), the NSW Cloud Policy ("cross-border storage, processing and access"), the NSW ICTA ("stored, processed, accessed, disclosed or viewed") and the NT checklist ("who provides IT support and where are they located?") would each classify the hybrid design as an offshore arrangement, because processing happens in London and UK support staff can see the data.
- **None of these is an absolute ban.** For OFFICIAL: Sensitive data, which identifiable patient health information would normally be (an assumption, not confirmed from a SA source), the hybrid and all-in-London designs both need the same approvals: an SA Accountable Authority risk acceptance, NSW agency risk assessment and CISO approval for ICTA cl 9.4 consent, and a WA Zone C risk assessment. The hybrid design might help a hospital's risk assessment marginally (data at rest in Australia; backups onshore). It does not remove the need for approval.
- **WA's zones are defined by where the service is "hosted".** A hybrid service could arguably be presented as "Australian-hosted" (Zone B) because storage is in Sydney. But the policy's own definitions cover "processing power" and information "stored and accessed". Presenting it as Zone B without disclosing UK processing would be misleading, and any honest classification probably lands nearer Zone C.

## Q5. The Commonwealth Hosting Certification Framework (DTA): does "Certified Strategic" or data sovereignty apply to state hospitals or private vendors?

### Takeaway
The Hosting Certification Framework (HCF) is a Commonwealth procurement policy for non-corporate Commonwealth entities hosting sensitive government data, whole-of-government systems and PROTECTED systems. It does not bind state or territory public hospitals or private hospitals. It applies to a vendor only if the vendor sells hosting to Commonwealth agencies. Even Certified Strategic does not require Australian ownership.

### Cited Findings
- The Hosting Certification Policy defines "Commonwealth Entity" as a non-corporate Commonwealth entity. It applies to "Australian Government customers procuring hosting arrangements for sensitive government data, whole-of-government systems and systems rated at the classification level of PROTECTED". "Strategic Certification represents the highest level of assurance and allows the Australian Government to specify ownership and control conditions"; Assured Certification provides safeguards "through financial penalties against significant changes to ownership, controls and operations". — [Hosting Certification Framework – Framework overview (search summary)](https://www.hostingcertification.gov.au/node/6)
- HCF requirements applied to new contracts and extensions from 30 June 2022, after the Framework's 2021 release. — [DTA, Release of the HCF (search summary)](https://dta.gov.au/news/release-hosting-certification-framework)
- Law firm reading (17 June 2021): the Framework applies to Commonwealth agencies and is "not explicitly extended to states or the private sector". It "is not intended to exclude all forms of foreign investment and ownership – meaning Australian ownership or control is not a requirement to achieve even the highest certification level." — [Holding Redlich](https://www.holdingredlich.com/data-sovereignty-and-the-digital-transformation-agency-s-hosting-certification-framework)

### Inferences
- The HCF is not a legal or policy requirement for a state public hospital or private hospital customer of Poised. It may show up indirectly if a state health department copies its language into tenders, or if a Commonwealth-funded program contract (for example Department of Health and Aged Care programs) imposes its own data-location clauses.
- AWS itself is the relevant hosting provider for HCF purposes. Poised's own certification would matter only for Commonwealth customers.

- I could not open hostingcertification.gov.au directly (timeouts), so the current (2024+) version of the Hosting Certification Policy was not read. I did not check whether its scope changed after 2021–22.

## Q6. Private hospitals (for example Ramsay, Healthscope): sector rules or practice

### Takeaway
Private hospitals are APP entities under the Privacy Act. In NSW, Victoria and the ACT they are also bound by state health records laws. None of these requires onshore storage. Australia's largest private operator, Ramsay, publicly says it may store information outside Australia, which shows offshore storage is lawful and practised. Enterprise private hospital practice is to prefer Australian regions and customer-controlled encryption, which is a contract and risk matter, not a legal mandate.

### Cited Findings
- Ramsay Health Care Australia's privacy policy summary (APP-bound): "We may outsource information and data storage services (including archiving of medical records), which may involve storing that information outside of Australia." And: "Where we outsource our services we take reasonable steps in the circumstances to ensure that third parties, including organisations outside of Australia, have obligations under their contracts with Ramsay to comply with all laws relating to the privacy (including security) and confidentiality of your personal information." — [Ramsay Health privacy policy summary](https://ramsayhealth.com/en/privacy-policy-summary)
- Ramsay chose Google Cloud as its data and innovation partner (Aug 2023), with press materials stating that only Ramsay controls access and use of patient data and that Ramsay uses its own encryption keys. — [Google Cloud press release](https://www.googlecloudpresscorner.com/2023-08-10-Ramsay-Health-Care-Joins-Forces-with-Google-Cloud-to-Help-Support-Its-2030-Digital-Strategy). This is a press release and should be read as marketing.
- NSW private health service providers must comply with the HRIP Act, including HPP 14. — [IPC NSW, For healthcare providers (search summary)](https://ipc.nsw.gov.au/healthcare-providers)
- The NSW, Victorian and ACT health records laws impose additional requirements on private health service providers beyond the Privacy Act. — [ALRC Report 96 (search summary)](https://www.alrc.gov.au/publication/essentially-yours-the-protection-of-human-genetic-information-in-australia-alrc-report-96/07-information-and-health-privacy-law/state-and-territory-privacy-legislation/)

### Inferences
- **For a private hospital customer, the binding test is APP 8** (plus HPP 14 or HPP 9 in NSW and Victoria). Both the hybrid and all-in-London designs can comply with an APP 8.1-compliant contract: APP-equivalent handling, subcontractor flow-down, breach notification, audit rights, deletion and return.
- **Commercially, large private groups will ask in vendor security questionnaires** where data is stored, where it is processed, where support staff sit, and whether they control the encryption keys. "Stored in Sydney, processed in London" must be disclosed accurately. The hybrid design is unlikely to satisfy a buyer who insists on Australian residency for its own risk reasons, because that buyer would usually also mean processing and access.

### Gaps
- No public Healthscope, St Vincent's Private, Healthe Care, Calvary or similar vendor data-location requirement was found.
- There is no published private-hospital industry standard (for example from the APHA or Catholic Health Australia) on vendor data location.
- Ramsay's procurement terms are not public.

## Q7. Is "remote access by foreign staff" or "processing in another country" treated differently from storage anywhere in Australia?

### Takeaway
Yes, but in the opposite direction from the user's belief. Wherever an Australian rule addresses location, it treats processing and access as at least as important as storage, and often makes access the trigger:
- **MHR Act s 77:** "process or handle… outside Australia".
- **OAIC APP 8:** disclosure occurs when the overseas recipient "is able to access or modify".
- **OVIC:** "stores or processes".
- **NT guidance:** "who provides IT support and where are they located?".
- **SA ruling:** "stored or processed offshore", including "remote ICT support… (if accessing data)".
- **NSW Cloud Policy and ICTA:** "storage, processing and access"; "stored, processed, accessed, disclosed or viewed".

The only context where location is treated as neutral is data merely routed in transit (OAIC 8.10).

### Cited Findings
- "process or handle the information relating to the records outside Australia" and "cause or permit another person… to process or handle…" (s 77(1)(b), (c)(ii)). — [My Health Records Act 2012](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2012A00063/latest/text)
- "there would not usually be a disclosure until the personal information is able to be accessed or modified by the overseas recipient… routing personal information, in transit, through servers located outside Australia, would usually be considered a 'use'" (para 8.10). Overseas parent technical support with database access is a disclosure (para 8.13). — [OAIC ch 8](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- "a provider that stores or processes information outside Victoria" is "a transborder transfer". — [OVIC IPP 9](https://ovic.vic.gov.au/?p=13934)
- "If the provider, or the hardware used by that provider, is not located in the Territory (or Australia)…"; "who provides IT support and where are they located?"; and a cloud contract that "allows the cloud provider to access the information… will be a disclosure." — [NT OIC guideline](https://infocomm.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0017/425510/5-Privacy-Cloud-Computing-May-2017-V1.pdf)
- "may only be stored or processed offshore…"; "ICT arrangements: Includes: cloud services, data processing… system monitoring, remote ICT support and maintenance (if accessing data)…"; a risk factor: "ability to vet staff located offshore with access to data". — [SACSF Ruling 2.1](https://security.sa.gov.au/cyber-security/sacsf/sacsf-ruling-2.1-offshore-data-storage-and-processing)
- "assess data residency and jurisdictional risks… including cross-border storage, processing and access". — [NSW Cloud Policy 2026 s 2.2.1](https://digital.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-09/nsw-government-cloud-policy-2026-v1.pdf)
- ICTA cl 9.4: Customer Data "stored, processed, accessed, disclosed or viewed by the Supplier outside of NSW" requires assessment and CISO approval (search snippet). — [NSW ICTA](https://www.info.buy.nsw.gov.au/__data/assets/word_doc/0009/993906/ICT-Purchasing-Framework-ICTA-agreement_December2024.docx)
- WA Health: entities "must have a clear understanding of the classification of information that will be stored and accessed". — [WA Health Cloud Policy](https://www.health.wa.gov.au/~/media/Corp/Policy-Frameworks/Digital-Health/Cloud-Policy/Cloud-Policy.pdf)

### Inferences
- **No Australian source found treats "data at rest in Australia, processed and accessed from abroad" as equivalent to onshore hosting.** The regulators' tests focus on who can access or modify the data and where they are. Putting only Postgres and S3 in Sydney therefore does not change the legal character of the arrangement under any Australian regime reviewed.

### Gaps
- No court or tribunal decision was found that applies these provisions to split storage and compute architectures.
- Encryption is mentioned in an OVIC case study as relevant to IPP 9. Whether customer-held keys (with London compute unable to decrypt without them) would turn the vendor into a mere "storage" provider was not researched. In practice, compute needs plaintext, so the point is likely moot for this design.

## Q8. Overall verdict: (a) storage in Australia, (b) also onshore-only processing and access, or (c) neither, only cross-border safeguards? Do the hybrid design and all-in-London comply for public and private hospitals?

### Takeaway
For ordinary hospital patient data, Australian **binding law is (c): no storage or processing localisation, only cross-border (and, in most states, cross-state) transfer and disclosure safeguards** that a contract can satisfy. The one statutory exception is the My Health Records Act s 77, which is (b) and covers processing and handling. It binds only registered My Health Record participants (System Operator, repository operators, portal operators, contracted service providers), not a vendor that doesn't connect. **State government policy layers (SA, NSW, WA) are risk-approval regimes** that explicitly cover offshore processing and remote access, not just storage. The user's belief that residency rules are only about storage, never processing, is **incorrect for Australia**: where location rules exist, they cover processing and access. The hybrid design gives no legal advantage over all-in-London. Both are lawful with the right contract and customer approvals, and both are caught by the same policy approvals.

### Cited Findings
- No localisation: APP 8 is a "reasonable steps" plus accountability framework intended "to facilitate the free flow of information across national borders". — [OAIC ch 8, paras 8.1–8.2](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-8-app-8-cross-border-disclosure-of-personal-information)
- Statutory localisation, including processing, is confined to the s 77 entity list. — [My Health Records Act 2012 s 77](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2012A00063/latest/text)
- State transfer principles are permissive on contract and reasonable-steps grounds: [NSW Health Privacy Manual s 13.2](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/policies/manuals/Documents/privacy-section-13.pdf); [Vic HPP 9](https://www.rch.org.au/uploadedFiles/Main/Content/ethics/HPP%20Extract.pdf); [Qld IP Act s 33](https://www.legislation.qld.gov.au/view/whole/html/inforce/current/act-2009-014); [Tas PIPP 9](https://www.legislation.tas.gov.au/view/whole/html/inforce/current/act-2004-046); [WA IPP 9 summary](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/office-of-the-information-commissioner/information-privacy-principles-summary); [NT OIC guideline](https://infocomm.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0017/425510/5-Privacy-Cloud-Computing-May-2017-V1.pdf)
- Policy layers that cover processing and access: [SACSF Ruling 2.1](https://security.sa.gov.au/cyber-security/sacsf/sacsf-ruling-2.1-offshore-data-storage-and-processing); [NSW Cloud Policy 2026](https://digital.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-09/nsw-government-cloud-policy-2026-v1.pdf); [WA Health Cloud Policy](https://www.health.wa.gov.au/~/media/Corp/Policy-Frameworks/Digital-Health/Cloud-Policy/Cloud-Policy.pdf)
- Private sector offshore storage is practised by the largest private hospital operator. — [Ramsay privacy policy summary](https://ramsayhealth.com/en/privacy-policy-summary)

### Inferences

**Separation of layers**

| Layer | What it requires | Covers storage? | Covers processing/access? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Binding law: Privacy Act APP 8 / s 16C** (private hospitals; Commonwealth) | Reasonable steps (contract) before disclosure to an overseas recipient; deemed liability | No location rule | Trigger is the overseas recipient's ability to access or modify |
| **Binding law: MHR Act s 77** (registered MHR participants only) | No holding, taking, processing or handling outside Australia | Yes | Yes, expressly |
| **Binding law: state transfer principles** (HPP 14 NSW, HPP 9 Vic, s 33 Qld, IPP 9 WA, PIPP 9 Tas, IPP 9 NT, TPP 8 ACT) | Transfer outside the state or Australia only on listed grounds (contract or reasonable steps suffice) | No storage rule; storing outside the state is itself a "transfer" (OVIC, NT) | Yes (OVIC "stores or processes"; NT and Qld control or access tests) |
| **Regulator guidance** (OAIC, OVIC, NT OIC, IPC NSW, NSW Health Manual) | Explains use vs disclosure; treats access as the trigger | — | Yes |
| **Government policy** (SACSF 2.1; NSW Cloud Policy 2026; WA Health MP 0140/20) | Risk assessment plus executive approval for offshore; SA recommends onshore | Yes | Yes (SA expressly includes remote support access; NSW "processing and access") |
| **Procurement and contract** (NSW ICTA cl 9.4; Commonwealth HCF for Commonwealth only; private hospital questionnaires) | Customer consent, CISO approval, location clauses | Yes | Yes ("stored, processed, accessed, disclosed or viewed") |

**Compliance judgment (these are inferences, not legal advice)**

1. **Hybrid (DB and S3 in Sydney; compute and support in London)**
   - *Public hospital, binding law:* **Can comply.** The UK vendor is an overseas recipient (or an overseas transfer) under every state principle because it can access the data. The hospital must rely on the contract / reasonable-steps ground (Qld: two limbs, for example "necessary for functions" plus "reasonable steps" or "contract substantially similar").
   - *Public hospital, policy:*
     - **SA:** needs a risk assessment and Accountable Authority acceptance, *exactly as for all-in-London*, because the data is "processed offshore" and remote support accesses it.
     - **NSW:** a cross-border "processing and access" risk assessment under the 2026 Cloud Policy, and ICTA cl 9.4 consent with CISO approval.
     - **WA Health:** risk assessment; arguably Zone C.
   - *Private hospital:* **Can comply** with an APP 8.1-compliant contract (plus HPP 14 or HPP 9 in NSW and Victoria). s 16C makes the hospital liable for Poised's breaches unless Poised has an Australian link.
   - *If integrated with My Health Record as a registered portal operator or contracted service provider:* **Does not comply** with s 77, because processing and handling in the UK is prohibited, as is "permitting" UK support access.

2. **All-in-London**
   - *Public hospital, binding law:* **Can comply** on the same grounds as the hybrid. Legally the analysis is the same.
   - *Public hospital, policy:* the same approvals as the hybrid. Approval may be somewhat harder to obtain, because storage and backups are also offshore (SA risk factors include foreign jurisdiction over data holdings and State Records Act compliance). The SA OCIO recommendation to "process and store" in Australia is met by neither design.
   - *Private hospital:* **Can comply** with an APP 8 contract.
   - *My Health Record integration:* **Does not comply.**

3. **Practical implication for Poised's architecture decision**
   - The hybrid design adds cross-region latency on every query and operational complexity. It does not move Poised out of any Australian regime that all-in-London is in.
   - Its only plausible benefits are:
     - a marginally better risk-assessment narrative: data at rest, backups and logs onshore; easier return and deletion; State Records storage location;
     - honestly answering "data stored in Australia" in questionnaires. But NSW, SA, NT and WA instruments expressly ask about processing and access as well.
   - If an Australian customer genuinely requires residency (for example SA's onshore recommendation, a NSW CISO who declines offshore consent, or future My Health Record integration), the requirement will almost certainly include onshore compute and onshore or tightly controlled support access. In practice that means a full Australian deployment (Sydney, or Melbourne for Victorian state-border optics), with UK support access either removed or made break-glass, logged and approved.

### Gaps
- No Australian law firm article was found that analyses split storage and compute architectures specifically. The conclusions above apply the OAIC, OVIC, NT and SA texts directly.
- The current text of the NSW HRIP Act, WA PRIS Act sch 1, Vic HRA and ACT HRPA should be checked on the official sites before relying on exact wording.
- It is not confirmed whether any NSW Health, Queensland Health or Victorian Department of Health internal directive imposes an onshore-only rule for clinical SaaS. Hospital procurement teams should be asked directly.
- Whether the UK will be (or has been) prescribed under the new APP 8.2 prescribed-countries exception is unknown. If it is, private-hospital APP 8 compliance would become simpler for both designs.
